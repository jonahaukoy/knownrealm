Daeron Targaryen

House Targaryen

Daeron the Drunken: Maekar's eldest, a soft, amiable man who wants nothing to do with swords or crowns — only a full cup. His dreams, though, have an unsettling way of coming true; he dreamed a dragon fell at Ashford.

The fuller tale

Maekar’s eldest son inherits none of his father’s hardness and none of his uncle Baelor’s ambition — Daeron the Drunken, as the smallfolk take to calling him with more affection than the nickname might suggest, wants nothing more complicated out of life than a full cup and an excuse not to be handed a sword. He is soft, amiable, and by his own cheerful admission entirely unsuited to either knighthood or kingship, a disposition that in a lesser-placed man might simply be eccentricity and in a Targaryen prince reads, to some at court, as a quiet scandal.

What complicates the easy dismissal of him is that his dreams have an unnerving habit of coming true. Before Ashford Meadow’s tourney has even properly begun, Daeron dreams that a dragon falls there — an image that reads, once Prince Baelor lies dying of a trial’s wounds, less like a drunkard’s fancy than an unwanted prophecy, and one Daeron himself seems no more comfortable holding than anyone else who hears it repeated afterward.

He drifts through the tales as a figure of comic relief that never quite manages to stay comic — genial company at a feast, useless in any crisis requiring nerve or a blade, and, by simple default, one fewer obstacle between his youngest brother and a throne that none of Maekar’s other sons particularly want or particularly deserve. History remembers him mostly for what he declines to become, which turns out, in a family stalked by ambition and madness in roughly equal measure, to be a strange kind of mercy.

The page and the screen

Henry Ashton’s Daeron rounds out the show’s quartet of princes with the season’s lightest touch, providing comic relief that the source material also leans on without ever losing sight of the character’s stranger edge — the prophetic dream sequence, when it arrives, lands with more unease on screen than a simple drunkard’s aside would suggest, matching the novella’s own quiet insistence that Daeron’s dreams are not to be laughed off entirely.
