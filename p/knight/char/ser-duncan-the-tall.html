Ser Duncan the Tall

The Hedge Knights

Dunk: a boy of Flea Bottom who grew nearly seven feet tall, squire to old Ser Arlan of Pennytree since childhood. Slow to anger, honest to a fault, and armed with the old man's sword, shield, and lessons — 'sworn shield of the weak, protector of the innocent,' whether he ever heard the words said over him or not.

The fuller tale

No maester wrote down the day Duncan of Flea Bottom became a knight, and that absence is the whole of his early life in miniature. A gutter child grown enormous on scraps and stubbornness, he fell in with an aging hedge knight named Arlan of Pennytree somewhere on the roads out of King’s Landing and simply never left his service — carrying his shield, currying his horses, and growing, year by year, into a young man built like a castle gate. Whether Ser Arlan ever spoke the actual words of knighthood over him is a question Dunk avoids asking himself too closely; by the time it might have mattered, the old man’s memory had already begun to fail.

What he inherits, when Ser Arlan dies of a chill on the road, is not a title so much as a set of tools and a decision. He has a sword, a shield, two unimpressive horses, and a boyhood’s worth of half-remembered lessons in courtesy and the lance — and he chooses, over the grave he digs with his own hands, to keep going to Ashford Meadow as a knight rather than turn back to Flea Bottom as nobody at all. It is the least dramatic possible staging for the beginning of a legend: a tall, poor, illiterate young man deciding that a title nobody can verify is nonetheless one he intends to earn honestly from that day forward.

Ashford gives him the tale’s defining test within days of his arrival. Watching Prince Aerion Brightflame break the fingers of a puppeteer’s boy for the crime of a puppet show he disliked, Dunk does the one thing every wiser head around him knows better than to do — he knocks a Targaryen prince into the dirt in front of witnesses. The trial of seven that follows, in which the greatest knights in the Seven Kingdoms must fight and possibly die to settle whether a hedge knight’s word means anything, is less a legal proceeding than a referendum on the realm’s own claimed values. Dunk keeps his hand and his foot. He gains, in the bargain, a debt to Prince Baelor Breakspear that he will carry for the rest of his life, and a squire — a bald, sharp-tongued stableboy who chose him before he had any idea what he was choosing.

The years that follow, chronicled in the drought at Standfast and the wedding at Whitewalls, are an education in everything the songs about knighthood leave out. Sworn to Ser Eustace Osgrey, an old man fighting a war that ended a generation ago, Dunk learns that honoring an oath does not require the lord who holds it to be wise, only that the man who swore it be steady. At Whitewalls he walks unknowingly into the last gasp of a rebellion, and does what he has always done when the choice narrows to plain and hard: he draws steel for the people standing nearest to him and worries about the politics afterward, if there is an afterward.

Across all three tales he remains, by his own frequent and gloomy estimate, no one’s idea of a great knight — too poor for a proper horse, too honest for court, too large to go unnoticed and too plain-spoken to charm his way out of the notice he gets. What the stories build, quietly and without any of the fanfare given to princes, is the case that these are exactly the qualifications that matter. He becomes, within the tales’ own logic and within the wider history the show and books both gesture toward, the rarest thing Westeros produces: a knight whose oath was never inherited, purchased, or coerced, but chosen, alone, over a grave by a road.

The dynasty he serves does not yet know what to do with him, which suits him. He outlives kings whose names his own grandchildren would have known better than his; the boy he grudgingly took as a squire because he had nowhere else to put him becomes a king in his own right one day. History remembers Ser Duncan the Tall as a hedge knight who never stopped being one even after the Iron Throne itself came to depend on his judgment — a fact that would have baffled the boy digging a grave outside Ashford Meadow, and delighted him too, once he’d finished being embarrassed about it.

The page and the screen

A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms premiered on HBO in January 2026 as a direct, largely faithful adaptation of The Hedge Knight, with Peter Claffey’s Dunk drawing the kind of notices — critics cited his stillness as much as his size — that suggested the production understood the character’s central trick: he is compelling precisely because he underreacts to everything except cruelty. The season kept the trial of seven as its emotional spine, and the show’s decision to let the fight play out at length, rather than compress it into a montage, matched the novella’s own patience with violence and its cost.

Where the season necessarily expands is atmosphere — Flea Bottom, Ashford Meadow’s tent city, the texture of hedge-knight poverty — since a novella’s compression has to become six hours of television somehow. The bones of the story, though, arrived largely untouched: readers of The Hedge Knight found their trial of seven, their puppet show, and their reluctant new squire essentially where they left them, which is more than most adaptations of beloved short fiction manage.
