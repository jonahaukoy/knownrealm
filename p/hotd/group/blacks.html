The Blacks — the Queen's Party

The party of Princess — then Queen — Rhaenyra: the painted table's council of dragons and ships. The Velaryon fleet, the Vale, the North and the riverlands answer to the black banners.

The fuller tale

The Blacks take their name, and much of their identity, from a single afternoon: the black gown Rhaenyra is said to have worn to a council convened to discuss the very question of her own succession, set against the green her rival wore to the same room. Grand Maester Munkun’s chronicle turns the color into shorthand for the whole faction almost immediately, and by the time the war is a year old, black has stopped being a wardrobe choice and become a banner.

What the color organizes is, in practice, the bulk of the realm’s naval and outlying strength: the Velaryon fleet that Corlys built into the richest navy in Westeros, the Vale’s ships and mountain refuge under Jeyne Arryn, the North’s eventual weight under Cregan Stark, and riverlords like the Blackwoods who see in Rhaenyra’s cause a chance to settle older, more local scores under royal cover. It is a coalition assembled from the edges of the map inward, in contrast to a rival faction rooted in the crownlands and the Reach.

The faction’s defining gamble is the Red Sowing at Dragonstone — a desperate call for any claimant with a drop of Valyrian blood, highborn or not, to try the three dragons left riderless by the war’s early losses. It costs lives, burns several hopefuls outright, and produces, against every rule the Targaryens had quietly kept for a century, dragonriders with no family name worth mentioning — a wager that changes what a dragon rider is allowed to look like, permanently, whether the Greens like it or not.

By the war’s last year the Blacks are less a single court than a coalition running on momentum and grief — a queen dead, a king dead, and armies converging on King’s Landing more out of accumulated obligation than any single living leader’s command. That the coalition still wins the crown for a Targaryen line, if not for the queen it started the war to crown, says as much about the strength of what Rhaenyra built as it does about what the fighting cost her personally.

The page and the screen

The show renders the black-versus-green council scene as the war’s clean point of origin — two dresses, two colors, a single afternoon that explains everything after it. Fire & Blood is less tidy about it: the color-coding is explicit at that one council and then applied more loosely and retroactively by the chroniclers describing everything that follows, which means the book’s Dance feels, on the page, considerably messier and less choreographed than the visually clean binary the screen version commits to from its opening scenes onward.
