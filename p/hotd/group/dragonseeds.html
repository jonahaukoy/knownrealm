The Dragonseeds

Bastards and smallfolk with the blood of old Valyria in them, summoned by the queen to try the riderless dragons. Dozens burned; a handful rose — and changed the war's arithmetic.

The fuller tale

The Red Sowing is the Blacks’ answer to a problem no amount of ships or riverlords could solve on their own: three dragons at Dragonstone with no rider, and a war being decided, increasingly, by whoever has more of them in the air. Rhaenyra’s solution breaks a rule the dynasty had quietly kept for a century — that dragons answer to Valyrian blood of acknowledged, legitimate descent — by throwing the call open to anyone in the city who can claim so much as a rumor of dragon ancestry, highborn or gutter-born alike.

The Sowing is less a ceremony than a culling. Dozens come forward to try Vermithor and Silverwing, dragons old enough and short-tempered enough to kill outright rather than merely refuse a rider, and several candidates burn in front of the whole assembled crowd before anyone succeeds — a public, repeated demonstration that dragon blood is not a courtesy title but a physical fact some claimants simply do not possess.

The ones who do succeed change the war’s arithmetic and its politics at the same time: Hugh Hammer claims Vermithor, the Bronze Fury, second-oldest living dragon in the world; Ulf White claims Silverwing; and Addam of Hull, a shipwright’s son with more claim to Velaryon blood than anyone had previously bothered to acknowledge, claims Seasmoke. None of the three had a title worth mentioning a season earlier, and all three are, by the Sowing’s end, riders of dragons great lords would have killed for.

Outside the Sowing’s official roll entirely, a young woman known only as Nettles claims the wild dragon Sheepstealer near Driftmark on her own initiative, unsanctioned and unexpected — proof, alongside the Sowing itself, that the war’s single greatest disruption to a century of Targaryen orthodoxy was simply discovering how many people the old rules had been quietly excluding all along.

The page and the screen

Nettles’s fate afterward is one of Fire & Blood’s most openly disputed episodes — Mushroom’s account has Daemon killing her in a fit of jealous rage after suspecting an affair with the dragon rather than the woman, while other sources have her simply flying away from the war entirely and surviving it, unresolved. The show has to commit to a single version for its ending; the book, characteristically, refuses to tell you which chronicler to believe.
