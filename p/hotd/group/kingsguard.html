The Kingsguard

The seven white cloaks sworn to shield the king — an oath the Dance splits down the middle, brother against brother, queensguard against kingsguard.

The fuller tale

Sworn for life is the order’s whole premise — seven knights in white cloaks, oaths that outlast any single reign, loyalty meant to sit above politics rather than inside it. Aegon the Conqueror founded the order on the assumption that there would only ever be one king to protect at a time; the Dance is the first crisis in the dynasty’s history to test what happens when that assumption simply stops being true.

It fails the test the same night Viserys dies. Ser Criston Cole throws his white cloak behind Aegon’s coronation and becomes the Greens’ most effective sword; other members of the order stay with Rhaenyra rather than the crown that has just changed hands out from under her, an act the chronicles struggle to even name cleanly, since a Kingsguard sworn to a queen who has not yet been crowned exists in a gap the order’s own vows never anticipated.

No single episode captures the order’s collapse more starkly than the Cargyll twins — Erryk and Arryk, indistinguishable in white cloaks and near-identical faces, ending up on opposite sides of the same war and meeting, at last, in single combat neither one survives, each killed by the brother he was raised beside rather than by any enemy either of them chose. Steffon Darklyn, for his part, carries Viserys’s own crown to Rhaenyra at Dragonstone as an act of loyalty the split order can still occasionally manage, before dying defending her cause when the Dragonpit itself is stormed by a mob that answers to neither white cloak nor black banner.

What the order looks like by the war’s end is less a single sworn brotherhood than a scattering of individual, contradictory oaths — proof that sworn for life was always a promise about duration, never about which king, or queen, a given knight would end up dying for.

The page and the screen

The Cargyll twins’ duel is staged directly on screen in the show, choreographed and unambiguous, brother killing brother in a corridor with the audience given every blow. Fire & Blood’s account is secondhand and reconstructed after the fact — no chronicler was in the room, and the book has to describe two men found dead together rather than show the fight that killed them, leaving the duel’s exact particulars, like so much else in the source material, a matter of informed reconstruction rather than eyewitness record.
