Criston Cole

The Greens — the King's PartyThe Kingsguard

A steward's son of the Dornish Marches who won a tourney and a white cloak with it — the finest sword of his generation, sworn shield to the princess, and the man the realm will call Kingmaker. His vows fit him worse than his armor.

A soul born at Blackhaven, in the Dornish Marches, sworn to The Greens — the King's Party, The Kingsguard.

The fuller tale

Season One

In Second of His Name — The Dornish marches' finest sword, sworn to the princess's side. Rhaenyra rides out angry, kills a boar in the dark with Ser Criston, and watches a white hart — the king's omen — walk away unkinged. In King of the Narrow Sea — A white cloak left on the floor of a locked room. What starts in the pleasure house Daemon cannot finish; what Rhaenyra starts that night with Ser Criston, she does.

In We Light the Way — Confesses to the queen and finds a protector where he sought a headsman. At the betrothal feast Ser Criston, refused as a paramour and half mad with broken vows, beats Laenor's lover Ser Joffrey Lonmouth to death on the floor. Ser Harwin breaks his fist on Ser Criston's face defending his... the princess's sons, and is dismissed from the Watch to Harrenhal. In The Green Council — Kingmaker now, by his own hand and an old man's blood.

Old Lord Beesbury alone objects that this is treason; Ser Criston opens his throat on the council table.

Season Two

In Rhaenyra the Cruel — Hand of the King now — and author of the war's stupidest gambit. Aegon rages past his grandsire's counsel; Otto is stripped of the Hand's badge for the second time in his life, and Criston Cole takes it. In The Burning Mill — The Hand marches — a kingmaker learning what kings cost. Their story

Criston Cole arrives at court with nothing his birth was supposed to grant him — a steward's son from the Dornish Marches, landless and lowborn by the standards of a court obsessed with both — and wins a tourney and a white cloak with sheer skill with a lance, becoming, almost overnight, the finest sword of his generation and a genuine social anomaly: a smallfolk boy sworn to the Kingsguard, that most aristocratic of orders, purely on merit.

Sworn as shield to Princess Rhaenyra, he becomes, for a time, considerably more than her protector — a romantic entanglement the book and the show both treat as real, whatever its precise physical extent, and one that costs Criston his composure the moment Rhaenyra chooses policy over feeling and marries Laenor Velaryon instead. His response at the wedding tourney — beating Joffrey Lonmouth, Laenor's lover and a man who has guessed too much about Criston's own heart, half to death on the field — is his first public act of the violence his broken vows will keep producing.

From there his loyalties migrate entirely to the green faction, and Criston becomes Alicent's sworn knight and eventually the greens' most reliable sword and most unreliable political mind — capable, disciplined and utterly without Otto Hightower's patience for subtlety. He is instrumental in securing Aegon's coronation and, in the war's opening councils, one of the loudest voices for treating any dissent as treason requiring immediate blood, a position that gets Lyman Beesbury killed at the small council table under his own hand.

As the Dance escalates, Criston rises to command the greens' armies in the field, and the realm that once called him the finest knight of his generation begins calling him something considerably less flattering: the Kingmaker, a man whose entire career has been in service of crowns he did not personally choose to make. He fights hard and fights well, but the war he helps start keeps outrunning whatever honor he still tells himself he is defending.

His campaigns take him through Harrenhal and finally into the riverlands, where the war's momentum — and the riverlords' long, patient grudges against anyone marching through their territory uninvited — eventually catches up to him entirely. By the time his host reaches its final engagement, Criston Cole has fewer allies, fewer options and fewer illusions about his own cause than he had at any point since the tourney that made his name.

Fire & Blood spends considerably more ink cataloguing Criston Cole's sins than mourning his end, which the book treats less as tragedy than as the arithmetic of a man who broke his vows once, publicly, and then organized the rest of his career around justifying the choice to everyone including himself.

How it ends for them

On the page. Three arrows at the Butcher's Ball (F&B, ch. 5)

The Kingmaker marched from Harrenhal into the riverlords' trap at the Butcher's Ball. Scorned, surrounded, and done running, he put on his white cloak, walked out alone, and took three arrows rather than kneel. The histories spend more ink on his sins than his end.

The series has not yet marched Criston to his final battle; his fate remains ahead of the current seasons.

Fire & Blood sends him from Harrenhal into a riverlord trap the histories remember as the Butcher's Ball — surrounded, outnumbered, and out of any path that does not end in surrender or death. Criston Cole chooses neither in the way his enemies expect: he puts on his white cloak, walks out from his own lines alone rather than send men to die covering his retreat, and takes three arrows standing rather than kneel to the riverlords who have waited years for exactly this reckoning. The book allows him, in his very last act, something closer to the honor he spent the whole war failing to embody.

Their chapters, book by book

A Son for a Son Blood and Cheese come over the walls of the Red Keep with a purse of Daemon's gold and instructions that fit in five words. Queen Helaena is made to choose among her sons; they take Jaehaerys's head regardless. The greens answer with a mummer's Cargyll: twin meets twin at Dragonstone's gates and both die of it. Daemon takes Harrenhal without a blow; Bracken and Blackwood open the war of the rivers at the Burning Mill; and Ser Criston Cole, Hand and kingmaker, marches the green host into the crownlands.

Where the page and the screen part

The show, in its aired seasons, gives Criston's romance with Rhaenyra and his subsequent turn toward the greens considerably more screen time and emotional interiority than Fire & Blood affords him — the book relays his motivations mostly through the consequences of his actions, filtered through chroniclers who had little patience for a lowborn knight's wounded pride. Both versions agree on the essential arc: a man of genuine skill whose broken oath curdles into a career-long grievance he never stops re-litigating.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
