House Frey

Upjumped and numerous, the Freys hold the only bridge across the Green Fork, growing rich on the tolls of the Twins. Two blue towers joined by a bridge on grey, bannermen to the Tullys — and in this war, bought early: the black prince flies to their bridge himself to pay the toll in promises.

The fuller tale

The Freys are, by the standards of the Riverlands’ old blood, embarrassingly new money — a house that grew rich on tolls rather than conquest, holding the only crossing of the Green Fork and taxing every cart, army and rumor that needs to use it. We Stand Together is the motto; what the house actually stands on, more precisely, is the bridge, and the family has never pretended otherwise.

Nominally the Freys answer to Riverrun, but old Lord Grover Tully’s grip has gone soft by the time the war reaches the riverlands, and a house that has spent generations being condescended to by its social betters is not about to let a weakened liege dictate its wartime loyalties without extracting something for the privilege.

What the Blacks extract in return for that loyalty is speed: rather than wait for a courier and a treaty, Daemon Targaryen flies to the Twins himself, Caraxes landing at the one crossing that can make or break every riverlands campaign to come, and secures the bridge in person with promises rather than swords — favor, marriage, advancement, the currency a house of this pedigree wants most and has the least of on its own.

The bridge stays friendly for the length of the war, and the value of that single decision compounds with every army that crosses it unopposed — riverlands levies, and eventually the northern host and Vale ships converging on King’s Landing in the war’s final act, all of them owing the Freys nothing more than a toll they were only too glad to collect. Readers who know what this family becomes a century of grievance later may find the promises Daemon leaves at the Twins land rather differently than they were meant to.

The page and the screen

Daemon’s personal visit to secure the crossing is a moment the show can simply stage — dragon, bridge, bargain, in a single scene — while Fire & Blood reports the same alliance more as settled fact than dramatized negotiation, the kind of transaction the chronicle records in a sentence because no one thought to write down exactly what was promised in the room. The gap is less a contradiction than the usual difference between a history book and the camera that has to put someone inside it.
