House Lannister

The richest house of the sunset kingdoms until the Sea Snake sailed, lords of Casterly Rock and the gold beneath it. A golden lion on crimson, descended from Lann the Clever, they hold the Westerlands and command wealth enough to buy armies — this generation led by twins: Jason, who raises the banners, and Tyland, who counts the crown's coin.

The fuller tale

Casterly Rock is still, at the Dance’s opening, one of the two or three wealthiest seats in Westeros — gold enough under the hill to have bankrolled the realm more than once — though the house that mines it has recently had to watch a sea lord out-earn it for the first time in living memory. Hear Me Roar is the sigil’s boast; a Lannister always pays his debts is the saying with the sharper edge, and this generation is about to learn how it feels to be the one collecting rather than paying.

The Rock is ruled in this era by twin lords, Jason and Tyland, who divide the house’s ambitions neatly between them: Jason wants a crown of his own reflected glory, a marriage into the royal line and a war that lets the West prove its worth in the field; Tyland wants the ledgers, and takes the office of master of ships and coin in the green council itself, fighting the war from behind a desk in King’s Landing rather than from a saddle.

Jason gets his field and loses everything to it. He marches the western host east to link with the green armies gathering in the riverlands, meets Daemon Targaryen’s coalition of rivermen at the Red Fork, and dies there when his own line breaks — cut down, in the chronicle’s telling, by an unheralded squire the smallfolk immediately nickname Longleaf the Lionslayer, an ignoble end for a lord who wanted nothing more than a glorious one.

His death leaves the Westerlands leaderless at the worst possible hour, and Dalton Greyjoy’s ironborn — bought by the Blacks and unbothered by irony — take the opening, running their longships along an undefended coast and sacking Lannisport itself while the Rock’s heir is still a child too young to answer. For once in the story, the lions are the ones being reaved rather than doing the reaving.

Tyland’s war is quieter and, in the end, crueler: captured when the capital falls to Rhaenyra, he is handed to torturers hunting for the crown’s hidden treasury and kept in the dungeons through the worst of the war’s reversals, released only when the tide turns again — scarred badly enough that the marks outlast the war itself, and restored, eventually, to enough trust that he ends his career as Hand of the King, a Lannister who paid a debt the house never agreed to owe and collected on it anyway.

The page and the screen

Jason Lannister’s death changes killers between page and screen — Fire & Blood credits an obscure squire, Pate of Longleaf, with the kill and the mocking epithet that follows him, while the show hands the moment to Cregan Stark’s northmen instead, folding the West’s humiliation into the North’s growing role in the story rather than leaving it to an anonymous foot soldier the book never mentions again.
