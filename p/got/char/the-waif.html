The Waif

The Faceless Men

A pale, ageless acolyte of the House of Black and White, who trains new servants of the Many-Faced God in the arts of the faceless — patiently, mercilessly.

The fuller tale

In the House of Black and White, where names are surrendered at the door, she is only the waif — a slight, pale acolyte of the Many-Faced God who assists the temple’s priests and conducts its instruction. The books give her a history she offers, in the temple’s fashion, as possibly a lie: a Braavosi merchant’s daughter, poisoned slowly by a stepmother, given to the god by a father with nothing left to pay — and a body that stopped aging young, so that she looks a child and is not, a woman of thirty and more wearing a girl’s frame. Whatever the truth, she is the temple’s shape in miniature: soft-voiced, patient, and entirely without mercy, because mercy would imply a self to feel it.

To Arya Stark she is teacher first and antagonist always. It is the waif who administers the lying game — reading falsehood in a novice’s face and correcting error with a switch — and the waif who beats blind Arya in the temple yard, day upon day, in the show’s most compact image of the training’s cruelty. The show sharpens the relationship into open enmity: its Waif despises the interloper who will not surrender her name, and when Arya breaks the temple’s rules to spare a life, it is the Waif who petitions for, and receives, the assignment to end her. The hunt through Braavos that follows — the stabbing on the bridge, the chase through the streets — is the temple’s doctrine given legs: no one comes for the girl who insists on being someone.

Her function in the tale is to be the road Arya refuses. The Waif is what complete surrender to the House looks like — faceless, nameless, obedient, excellent — and the story sets her against a pupil who wants the skills without the annihilation. Their final confrontation settles the argument in the dark, on Arya’s chosen ground, and the temple receives its answer in the currency it deals in.

The page and the screen

The book Waif is no enemy: she is a small, sad, gentle presence who teaches Arya the tongue of Braavos and the craft of poisons, gives her the story of her own ruined childhood as a lesson in lies, and shows the novice nothing worse than the temple’s ordinary rigor. The lethal rivalry, the sanctioned hunt, and the duel in the dark are inventions of the show, which needed Braavos to end in something more cinematic than a curriculum. She is alive in the published books, still serving in the House of Black and White — which means the show killed a character the novels may still be using, one of the adaptation’s quieter unilateral choices.

The show: Her face joined the hall (S6·E8)

The faceless novice who hated the Stark girl hunted her one last time through Braavos — into a black cellar, where a blind girl's training and a single candle-cut ended the game. Her face joined the hall.

The show’s Waif dies in the sixth season, at the end of the hunt she asked to lead. Having stabbed Arya on the bridge and butchered the actress Lady Crane for sheltering her, she chases her quarry through Braavos — a pursuit staged as relentless, machine-like, unbeatable — into a bare cellar where Needle waits and a single sword-stroke puts out the candle. The show declines to stage the kill: the next thing seen is the Waif’s face, newly mounted in the hall of the House of Black and White, where Jaqen finds it and draws the intended conclusion. Arya beat her where the blind months had made Arya the native: in the dark. In the books the waif lives on in the temple, no man’s enemy — the duel belongs to the show alone.
