How it ends for them

On the screen. Fell in Meereen (alive in the books) (S5·E4)

Barristan the Bold, the finest sword of his age, died in a Meereenese alley with a dozen Sons of the Harpy dead around him. He fell defending the city of a queen he'd crossed the world to serve.

In the show Barristan dies in the fifth season, ambushed in the winding streets of Meereen when the Sons of the Harpy rise. Finding Grey Worm’s Unsullied cornered in an alley, the old Lord Commander joins the fight one final time, and the choreography is written as a thesis: one aging knight against a dozen masked insurgents, and it is nearly enough. He kills and kills, takes his wounds from behind and beside, and falls among a heap of his enemies — Barristan the Bold, dead on foreign stone for a queen he served barely two years, mourned by a court that only half understood what it had lost. His death hardens Daenerys toward Meereen’s old blood and shoves the whole storyline toward the fighting pits.

In the published books he is alive and at the height of his consequence — Hand of the Queen in Daenerys’s absence, leading Meereen’s sortie against the Yunkish siege as A Dance with Dragons ends. Whatever end Martin writes him, the show’s version bought its shock at the price of the character’s last act; the books are still spending it.

Where the page and the screen part

The divergence here is total after a point. In the books Barristan comes to Daenerys in disguise, as the humble squire Arstan Whitebeard attending the Astapor-bound merchant party — a deception that lasts a full book, ends in the dramatic double unmasking of himself and Jorah, and colors everything after; the show, reasonably, spends the disguise in a single scene and has him declare himself on a Qarth street. Far larger: book Barristan is alive, a POV character, and effectively the ruler of Meereen — Hand to an absent queen, jailer of Hizdahr, commander of the city’s defense as the fifth book closes. The show killed him in a fifth-season alley specifically to raise the Sons of the Harpy stakes, a choice that remains one of the adaptation’s most protested cuts, not least because the actor had reportedly been told his biggest material was still coming.

The books also give him one of the saga’s quietest running themes: an old man auditing his own legend, chapter by chapter, and finding that boldness was always the easy part — it was the standing post outside the wrong doors that stained. None of that interior audit survives to the screen; what survives is the sword, and the scene where he tells Daenerys the truth about her father, which both tellings agree no one else would have dared.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
