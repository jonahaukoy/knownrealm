Quentyn Martell

House Martell

Doran's quiet, dutiful elder son, unremarkable to look at, carrying a secret his father has kept for half a lifetime (book only).

The fuller tale

Quentyn Martell is the prince nobody would pick out of the line: Doran's elder son, square-built, plain, earnest, more comfortable with books than with the spear-and-dagger glamour his uncle's daughters wear like skin. Fostered with the Yronwoods — part hostage to an old blood-debt of Oberyn's, part bridge between Dorne's two proudest houses — he grew up dutiful in a family whose legends are all passionate, and the books weigh him constantly against the uncle he cannot resemble: the Viper's fire, the frog's patience. His father's plan gives him the heaviest errand in the saga: cross half the world in secret and bring back Dorne's hidden ally — a queen, and her dragons.

The mission is a pilgrimage of everything going wrong. His party sails as merchants and is unmasked; his closest friend dies of a corsair's blade on the way; the storied hired ship becomes a slog through the Demon Road's ruins; and to reach besieged Meereen at all, the heir to Dorne signs on with a sellsword company and scrubs pots under a false name, a prince of the blood learning war's actual texture from its cheapest seats. When he finally stands before Daenerys Targaryen with his marriage pact — the paper his father spent seventeen years protecting — the story delivers its quiet, brutal joke: he is years too late, she is pledged elsewhere for politics, and the queen is kind about it, which is somehow worse.

What he does next is the character's whole question. With the sensible options exhausted — go home, wait, accept — Quentyn decides that a Martell who crossed the world for fire and blood cannot return carrying neither, and that the dragon has no need of a marriage contract. The attempt on the pit where two dragons are chained is folly by every measure the books themselves supply; it is also, his chapters make clear, the first thing in his life Quentyn ever chose entirely for himself — the frog prince reaching for the crown the tale of it promised, in defiance of every signal that the tale was never about him.

The page and the screen

The show cut Quentyn without residue: no character inherits the mission, the pact, or the theme, and Dorne's connection to Daenerys arrives by Ellaria's diplomacy instead of a prince's corpse. What is lost is one of the books' most deliberate structures — a whole point-of-view arc built to interrogate the story's own romances: the secret prince, the quest, the destined marriage, each trope led out and shot in order. His absence also unmoors pieces of the books' Meereen endgame, where the consequences of his choices — for the dragons, the siege, and the Tattered Prince's bargain over Pentos — are still detonating when the published pages run out.

Before the tale: (book only)

The books: Burned reaching for a dragon (ADWD, ch. 70)

The quiet prince crossed half the world with a marriage pact to claim a dragon queen, and reached for Viserion's chains when she flew. The dragons answered fire with fire. He lived three days after; the books call him the frog prince who died far from home.

His death is book-only and among the saga's cruelest. With Daenerys vanished on Drogon's back and Meereen unraveling, Quentyn leads his two surviving friends and hired swords into the Great Pyramid's pit to steal a dragon, on the theory that the blood of old Valyria runs in Martell veins too. The theory is heard and rejected: in the darkness and panic Rhaegal's flame takes him full on, and the Tattered Prince's men scatter as the loosed dragons break free over the city — the mission's final gift to Meereen being two ungoverned monsters on its rooftops. Quentyn lingers three days in a bed, burned past recognition, his friend's hand in what remains of his; the chapter that ends him is titled, with the books' terrible economy, after the dragontamer he briefly was. The show never so much as speaks his name.
