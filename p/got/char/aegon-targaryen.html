Aegon Targaryen

House TargaryenSellswords & Free Companies

A silver-haired youth called Young Griff, raised in secret across the narrow sea and presented to the Golden Company as Rhaegar's living son — heir, they say, to the Iron Throne (book only).

The fuller tale

The boy was raised on a boat, which is the first thing to understand about him. The Shy Maid is a shabby poleboat working the Rhoyne, crewed by a hired sword called Griff, his blue-haired son Young Griff, a septa, a halfmaester, a squire called Duck — and the whole of it is a school in disguise, because the blue-haired lad is, his guardians believe and teach, Aegon Targaryen: son of Rhaegar and Elia, heir of the dragon, saved from the Sack of King’s Landing as an infant when Varys swapped a tanner’s baby into the royal cradle to meet the Mountain’s hands. For nearly two decades, while the world’s story ran elsewhere, the true king — if he is that — poled up and down a river, learning languages, arms, history and humility from the best tutors a conspiracy could buy.

He is, the books take care to show, a genuinely well-made prince: clever, lettered, brave, trained to rule by a curriculum Varys later describes with pride — a king who knows hunger, who has walked among the smallfolk, who was raised to duty rather than entitlement. He is also, the same chapters murmur, a seventeen-year-old with a seventeen-year-old’s vanity and temper, never once tested by anything he could actually lose. The test arrives wearing Tyrion Lannister’s face: the fugitive dwarf, ferried downriver with the conspiracy, sniffs out the boy’s identity within days, and in a cyvasse game laced with poison plants the idea that redirects the war — do not go east to beg Daenerys’s favor like a suitor with empty hands; go west, invade Westeros, and greet her as a king with a war of his own.

The advice is taken, which may be exactly what the advice-giver intended. Aegon and the Golden Company — the exile legion founded a century ago for Blackfyre pretenders, a lineage the books mention with a very straight face — turn their ships west while Daenerys sits in Meereen, and the landings in the stormlands succeed beyond hope: castles fall, storm-lords declare, and the boy proves himself in the storming of Storm’s End itself. As the published books end, a Targaryen banner flies over Durrandon walls, Dorne is weighing its old marriage-pact loyalties, and the realm — bled white by five kings — has not yet understood what has landed on its coast. The dragon has returned to Westeros, and it is not the one anyone was watching.

Over all of it hangs the question the books built him to carry: is he true? Daenerys’s visions at the House of the Undying warned of a mummer’s dragon, a cloth dragon on poles swayed before a cheering crowd; the conspiracy that raised him smells of Illyrio’s private griefs and the Blackfyre line that the Golden Company existed to crown; and every proof of his blood is the word of the men selling him. The books decline to answer. The boy himself does not doubt — which, the tale quietly suggests, is either the surest sign he is a king, or the surest sign he was built to be mistaken for one.

The page and the screen

Young Griff does not exist in the show — no swap in the Sack, no boat, no invasion — and the name Aegon Targaryen was reassigned to the adaptation’s own reveal: Jon Snow’s true name in the seventh-season parentage twist, an economy that let one Aegon do the narrative work of two. The consequences of the cut run deep: the show’s Varys becomes a simple Targaryen loyalist rather than the architect of a rival claimant; the Golden Company arrives late, fights for Cersei, and dies in a morning; and Daenerys reaches Westeros uncontested by the second dragon whose existence, in the books, reshapes her entire approach. In the published novels Aegon is alive, victorious and ascendant — master of Storm’s End with the realm stirring around him — and the fandom’s longest-running argument, real prince or Blackfyre mummer’s dragon, remains deliberately unsettled by the page.
