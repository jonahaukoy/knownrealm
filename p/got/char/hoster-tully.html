Their story

Family, Duty, Honor — Hoster Tully spent a lifetime arranging the riverlands around those three words, in that order. Lord of Riverrun in the last years of Targaryen rule, he governed a country with no mountains, no deserts and no walls: rivers run through it and armies run along rivers, so the Tullys have always survived by being everyone’s friend or everyone’s road. Hoster’s answer was marriage. His daughter Catelyn went north to the Starks, his daughter Lysa east to the Arryns, and the price of both matches was Riverrun’s swords for Robert’s Rebellion — a wedding-day treaty that helped pull down a three-hundred-year dynasty.

He was a shrewd, proud, fundamentally warm man who could be granite where duty was concerned. When his brother Brynden refused the marriage picked out for him, Hoster quarreled with him for decades rather than yield the point. When his ward Petyr Baelish — a minor lordling fostered at Riverrun as a courtesy — reached above his station toward Catelyn, Hoster shipped the boy home and considered the matter closed. It wasn’t, of course. Half the tale’s catastrophes trace back to decisions made in Hoster’s hall by a lord doing exactly what lords were supposed to do.

By the time the story opens he is already failing, eaten from within by a sickness no maester can turn, and the great war of his lifetime is fought across his rivers while he lies abed above it. Riverrun fills with his grandson’s banners; a king is proclaimed in his hall; the Kingslayer is held in his dungeon — and the Lord of Riverrun drifts in and out of milk of the poppy, mistaking his daughter for the dead and the dead for the living. The books give these bedside scenes to Catelyn, and they are among the quietest, saddest chapters in the saga: a strong man’s long subtraction, one memory at a time.

His meaning in the tale is the old order itself. Hoster played the game the way it had been played for a thousand years — daughters as treaties, wards as investments, tolls and bridges and balance — and every bargain he struck honestly became kindling for the fire that came after. The Frey marriage pact, the Arryn alliance, the fostered boy with the mockingbird pin: he built the machine in good faith, and lived just long enough to hear it begin to grind.

How it ends for them

On the screen. Died of long illness (S3·E3)

The old Lord of Riverrun died abed of the long sickness while the war he never wanted burned across his rivers. His funeral boat carried him down the Tumblestone with fire arrows for a farewell — the third of them, at last, well shot.

On the page. Died abed, given to the river (ASOS, ch. 46)

The Lord of Riverrun died slowly of the sickness in his belly while the war burned his rivers, calling out 'Tansy' for a wrong done long ago. Edmure fired three arrows over his funeral boat; the Blackfish's fourth wasn't needed.

In the show he dies of his long illness offscreen between seasons, and the third episode of the third season is his farewell: Riverrun gives its lord to the river in the old way, his body sent down the water in a burning boat. Edmure’s flaming arrows fall short again and again until the Blackfish takes the bow from his hands, and the scene doubles as a portrait of the family he left — the earnest heir who cannot quite manage, and the old soldier who does not stay to watch the shaft land.

The books tell it much the same, a chapter or two deeper: he dies abed at Riverrun in A Storm of Swords with Catelyn near, after months of poppy-dulled twilight, and the funeral boat goes down the river trailing fire while his sworn men watch from the walls. Both tellings agree on the essential mercy — of all the lords of the Trident, Hoster Tully alone was allowed to die of nothing worse than time.

Where the page and the screen part

Show viewers never meet Hoster alive: he exists as a funeral — one episode, one boat, one bow passed from nephew to great-uncle. The books keep him dying across two volumes, and give him one of the series’ buried tragedies: in his fever he mutters the word “Tansy,” an apology forty years old, for the moon tea he forced on an unmarried, pregnant Lysa and the choices he made for her afterward. That guilt reframes the entire Arryn storyline — much of what Lysa becomes was manufactured at Riverrun, by a father certain he was doing his duty.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
