Euron Greyjoy

House Greyjoy

The Crow's Eye: Balon's exiled brother, a swaggering, black-humored pirate king feared even by his own kin — the show's grinning terror of the seas.

The fuller tale

Euron Greyjoy arrives late in the tale and instantly reorganizes it around himself, which is exactly how he would want it described. Third of the Greyjoy brothers, banished by Balon years before the story starts for a crime against Victarion’s wife, he spent his exile as the most feared reaver in the known world — and comes home within a day of his brother’s convenient death, all smiles, to ask what the fuss is about. Where Balon was flint, Euron is quicksilver: charming, blasphemous, theatrical, and empty in a way that men mistake for depth until it is far too late to matter.

The show’s Euron announces himself on a rope bridge in a storm, killing his brother mid-sentence, and campaigns for the driftwood crown on a platform of remarkable frankness: yes, he did it, and what has anyone else here ever finished? He wins the kingsmoot over Yara and Theon by promising to build a thousand ships and marry the dragon queen, then discovers his niece and nephew have stolen his best fleet overnight. His response sets his tempo for the rest of the series — build more, sail faster, and fall on his enemies precisely where they feel safest.

What follows is the most efficient villain streak of the late seasons. He shatters Yara’s fleet in a night action lit by burning ships, kills two Sand Snakes with their own weapons, and drags his niece to King’s Landing as a gift for Cersei — a queen he courts with the cheerful persistence of a man who has decided the Iron Throne is a marriage away. He ferries the Golden Company across the Narrow Sea, ambushes Daenerys from behind a rock shadow and shoots Rhaegal out of the sky with a scorpion bolt, and beds the queen he serves. If the show’s Euron has a creed, he states it plainly enough: he wants everything, mostly because taking it from other people is the only thing that entertains him.

The books’ Euron is another order of being entirely. The Crow’s Eye, they call him: a blue-lipped drinker of shade-of-the-evening, one eye covered by a patch no man asks about, captain of a red ship called Silence crewed entirely by men whose tongues he has torn out. He claims to have sailed the Smoking Sea and walked the ruins of Valyria; he keeps Valyrian armor, captive warlocks, and a cracked dragon horn taller than a man, won — he says — from the ashes of the Doom. Where show-Euron wants a throne, book-Euron talks like a man who has seen something behind the world and means to introduce everyone to it.

His book campaign is conquest as dark theater. At the kingsmoot beneath Nagga’s ribs he trumps every claim with plunder and blasphemy, has the hellhorn blown until its blower dies of it, and promises the ironborn nothing less than dragons and the world entire. Crowned, he binds the captains with gifts and salt wives, sends Victarion east to fetch Daenerys — a wooing he fully expects to end in betrayal, his brother’s or his own — and hurls the Iron Fleet at the Reach, stripping the Shield Islands and setting his eye on Oldtown, where the Citadel keeps its glass candles and its secrets. The sample chapters ahead of the next book go darker still: priests lashed to prows, visions of a kraken-crowned god on a throne of skulls, a man preparing not a battle but a ritual.

Between the two versions, Euron is the tale’s best argument that adaptation is interpretation. The show sharpened him into a killer with a fleet; the books grow him toward something like an apocalypse with a sense of humor. What both agree on is the essential vacancy at his center — brother, god, queen, crown, none of it is the point. The point is that nothing has ever been able to tell Euron Greyjoy no and make it stick, and he has organized his entire life around keeping it that way.

In the books' telling

The Crow's Eye: Balon's exiled brother, blue-lipped from shade-of-the-evening, who claims to have sailed to Valyria itself. A sorcerer-pirate with a dragon-binding horn and crew of mutes, dreaming of far darker crowns than driftwood.

The page and the screen

No character diverges more between page and screen. The show’s Euron is a leather-clad corsair with quips — a knowing, almost modern rogue whose ambitions run to fleets, crowns and Cersei’s bed. The books’ Euron is a sorcerer-king in embryo: the shade-of-the-evening, the Valyrian plunder, the dragonbinder horn, the mute crew, the hints of an eldritch design behind the piracy — none of it survived the adaptation. Even his election differs in texture: the show’s kingsmoot is a brisk shoreline vote among three speakers, while the books stage a great assembly on Old Wyk beneath the bones of Nagga, claimants and priests and the horn’s scream, with Aeron’s whole faith arrayed against the Crow’s Eye and failing.

Their endings part company completely. Show-Euron dies on the beach beneath the falling Red Keep; book-Euron, alive and ascendant in the published volumes, has just baited the fleets of Oldtown into battle while he readies something worse. Readers waiting on The Winds of Winter regard him, with reason, as the books’ most dangerous living man south of the Wall.

The show: Fell on the beach at King's Landing (S8·E5)

The crow's eye shot a dragon from the sky and washed ashore in time to duel Jaime Lannister beneath the falling Red Keep. He died grinning at his own wound: 'I'm the man who killed Jaime Lannister.' He wasn't.

The show kills him with the same swagger he lived by. Thrown into the sea when Daenerys annihilates his fleet in Blackwater Bay, he washes ashore beneath the collapsing Red Keep just in time to find Jaime Lannister hurrying to reach Cersei — and attacks him for the pure sport of it, grinning through a duel amid the falling ash. He deals Jaime mortal wounds; Jaime drives a sword through his belly and leaves him. Euron dies alone on the sand, delighted with himself to the last, crowing that he is the man who killed Jaime Lannister. He wasn’t — Jaime dies of a falling city, holding his sister — but Euron never let truth spoil a good story.

In the published books he is alive, crowned, victorious and accelerating — no death scene awaits him yet, only the battle off Oldtown and whatever the horn and the visions are for. Whatever end Martin has planned, readers broadly agree the beach brawl was written for a much smaller man.
