Joanna Lannister

House Lannister

Tywin's beloved wife and cousin, the one person said to have ruled the great lord himself; she died bringing his youngest son into the world.

The fuller tale

Joanna Lannister died a year before the story's oldest flashback and governs it anyway. Tywin's cousin and then his wife, she was by every surviving account the one person who could manage the most managed man in Westeros — the court's joke, told carefully out of his hearing, was that Lord Tywin ruled the realm and Lady Joanna ruled Lord Tywin. She served as a companion to Princess Rhaella in her youth, drew the attention of King Aerys in ways the books allow to curdle into ugly rumor, and left the capital to preside over Casterly Rock as its true administrator while her husband ran the Seven Kingdoms. The marriage was that vanishing rarity in the saga, a great match that was also a love match; what warmth existed in Tywin Lannister appears to have been held in trust by his wife, and returned to no one after her.

She bore the golden twins and, a decade later, died bringing Tyrion into the world — and it is the deaths and half-lives that radiate from that childbed that make her one of the story's great absent causes. Tywin's grief metastasized into the coldness that shaped three children and, through them, a war; his lifelong cruelty to Tyrion is grief wearing armor, the unforgivable accusation that the son cost him the wife. Cersei hoards her few memories of her mother like contraband. Tyrion, who never had any, spends his life being punished for a loss he shares. The books give her one more haunting: in a vision in the House of the Undying — trimmed from the show — Daenerys glimpses fragments that readers have spent decades connecting to Joanna's shadow over the dynasty. She is the proof, planted at the root of House Lannister, that the family's famous debt-keeping began with a debt no one could ever pay.

The page and the screen

On screen Joanna is a name — invoked by Tywin at his most human, by Cersei at her most wounded, by Oberyn Martell recalling the Lannister visit to Casterly Rock that the books stage in his memory too — and never a face; the show cast no one and filmed nothing of her. The books hold considerably more: her friendship and rivalry at Rhaella's court, the persistent and pointedly unresolved rumors about Aerys's obsession with her, and through them one of the fandom's most durable theories about Tyrion's parentage — which the novels neither confirm nor extinguish. Where the show uses her simply as the hole in Tywin's chest, the books use her as that and as a live question about whose story the saga is actually telling.

Before the tale: Died before the story

Her death precedes the tale in both tellings and is disputed in neither: she died in childbed at Casterly Rock in 273 AC, delivering the malformed son the maesters did not expect to live. The infant survived; the mother did not. Aerys's recorded condolence — that the gods had taken Joanna to teach Tywin humility — is credited in the books with finishing whatever remained of the friendship between king and Hand, and the household's grief was institutionalized by its lord: from that day the Rock ran colder. Every version of the family's story, on page and screen alike, treats her death as its true beginning — the wound Tyrion is blamed for, the love Tywin buried, and the vacancy Cersei spent a lifetime trying to inherit.
