Tyrion Lannister

House Lannister

The Imp: a dwarf, and the cleverest of the Lannisters, which his father has never forgiven him. He drinks, he reads, and he knows things — the only weapons his family left him.

The fuller tale

Tyrion Lannister's mother died bringing him into the world, and his father spent the next four decades presenting the bill. Born a dwarf into the realm's most image-conscious house — stunted legs, mismatched features, and in the books a swirl of pale hair and one eye so dark it looks black — he grew up as the joke Tywin Lannister could not silence, the walking proof that gold cannot buy everything. Cersei tormented him from the cradle; Jaime, alone in the family, loved him without conditions. Denied the soldier's body his house worships, Tyrion armed himself with what was left on the shelf: books, wine, jokes, and an unsentimental grasp of how power actually functions, which turns out to be the rarest weapon in Westeros. His own summary is the saga's best self-portrait — he drinks, and he knows things.

The formative atrocity of his life happened at thirteen, and the show cut it, which changes everything downstream. In the books, young Tyrion rescued and secretly married a crofter's daughter named Tysha — two weeks of stolen happiness before Tywin found out, declared her a hired whore, and had his garrison teach the boy a lesson designed to kill the possibility of love in him permanently. It nearly worked. What survived was the character both tellings share: a man who leads with the joke because the alternative is the wound, who pays for affection because he has been taught it can't be freely his, and who nurses, under all the performance, the tale's most reliable sympathy for the broken. He builds a saddle for a crippled boy he barely knows, defends a bastard on the Wall, and never once mocks anyone for what they cannot help. Cripples, bastards and broken things — he coined the category and holds lifetime membership.

His first tour through the story is a picaresque conducted at knifepoint. Arrested by Catelyn Stark on the strength of Littlefinger's lie about a dagger, he talks his way out of the Eyrie's sky cells, wins his freedom in a trial by combat fought by a sellsword he charms into the job, and accidentally acquires both a bodyguard and the mountain clans of the Vale on the walk home. Tywin, briefly impressed despite himself, sends him to King's Landing as acting Hand — and Tyrion, given actual power for the first time in his life, turns out to be alarmingly good at it. He exiles Janos Slynt, outmaneuvers Cersei's spies, ships Myrcella to safety, and runs the capital's defense while Joffrey hides: the wildfire trap that burns Stannis's fleet on the Blackwater is his, and when the battle wavers, it is the dwarf who leads the sortie out the Mud Gate with half a face freshly cut away. He wakes demoted, scarred, stripped of credit, and written out of the songs — the first full payment of the story's cruelest recurring invoice, in which Tyrion does the indispensable thing and is hated for it.

Joffrey's wedding turns the invoice into a verdict. Handed the blame for the king's poisoning by a mother who has wanted him dead since his birth, Tyrion endures a show trial in which every grievance he ever earned and several he didn't are sworn against him — culminating in Shae, the lover he tried to send to safety, testifying that he and Sansa planned it together. His response is the series' most electric monologue: he confesses, not to the murder, but to the crime of being a dwarf, tells the court he wishes he had let Stannis burn them all, and demands trial by combat. Oberyn Martell volunteers, dances, and dies with his skull in the Mountain's hands. The night before the execution, Jaime frees him — and Tyrion, instead of running, climbs to the Tower of the Hand for the reckoning the whole saga has been holding its breath for.

In exile he bets what remains of himself on Daenerys Targaryen. Smuggled across the narrow sea in a crate, sold to slavers, and talked back out of it — his mouth remains undefeated even when the rest of him is cargo — he reaches the dragon queen with a proposition no one else alive can make: he knows how Westeros actually works, and she is the first ruler he has ever believed in. As her Hand he gets Meereen governed, negotiates with masters and priests, and delivers her the realm's best political mind — which proceeds, across the final seasons, to be wrong about nearly everything. His mercies misfire; his clever compromises are outrun by dragons and grief; every prediction he makes about Cersei is answered by Cersei being Cersei. The wisest man in Westeros ends the war as its most spectacular failure, and — characteristically — is the first to say so, hurling his Hand's pin down the steps of the ruined throne room.

And then, because the story has always trusted him with its summations, Tyrion is handed the ending. A prisoner awaiting judgment, he talks the assembled lords of Westeros into the realm's strangest succession — power to the boy with the best story and no capacity to want it — and is sentenced, by the king he just invented, to the punishment that is also the point: Hand of the King, again, cleaning up after power, forever. The last we see of him he is rearranging the small council's chairs and being interrupted mid-sentence, which after eight seasons of nobody listening to Tyrion Lannister is either an injustice or a homecoming. He is the tale's argument that wit is a form of courage, and its warning that intelligence in service of the wrong loyalties is just eloquent complicity — and he would appreciate that both readings end with him buying the next round.

What makes him the saga's center of gravity is that he is its point of translation. Kings and smallfolk, slavers and septons, wildlings and whores — Tyrion is the only character who can hold a conversation with every stratum of two continents, because he has been despised by all of them and pays attention the way only the perpetually underestimated do. George R. R. Martin has called him a favorite; the show made him its longest-serving protagonist; and the story keeps him alive through every purge for the simplest structural reason there is. Somebody has to see what the tale is actually about, and say it out loud, preferably over wine.

The page and the screen

The Tysha cut is the great divergence, and it detonates twice. In the books, Jaime frees Tyrion personally and — as a parting honesty — confesses that Tysha was never a whore: the marriage was real, the love was real, and the lesson was Tywin's lie. Tyrion answers by telling Jaime a lie of his own out of pure spite, poisoning the brothers' farewell, and goes up the ladder to his father with the truth burning in him; his last exchange with Tywin is not about Shae but about where whores go. The show, which erased Tysha after a single first-season mention, plays the escape as tender and the murder as grief — and consequently ships a gentler Tyrion into exile. Book five's Tyrion is a far darker passenger: drunk, suicidal, cruel to nearly everyone, wandering the Rhoyne with a legitimate hidden Targaryen claimant the show never cast, a dwarf girl named Penny whose kindness shames him, and a nose he no longer has — the books took the whole thing at the Blackwater, where the show settled for a scar.

His book arc is also, as of the published novels, unfinished business: he has never actually met Daenerys. He ends the fifth book newly signed to the Second Sons, maneuvering outside a plague-ridden Meereen under siege, his queen still a rumor on a hilltop. Everything the show gives him from the fighting pits onward — the Handship, the wars, the verdicts — is television's own invention, built on the books' promissory note that the cleverest Lannister and the last Targaryen belong in the same room.

In the show, season by season

Season One. In Winter Is Coming — Reads everything; drinks and knows things. In The Kingsroad — Slaps a prince, then goes to see the edge of the world. In Lord Snow — Pisses off the top of the Wall, as promised. In Cripples, Bastards and Broken Things — Stops at the wrong inn on the wrong night. In The Wolf and the Lion — Saves Catelyn in the hill-tribe ambush — into a sky cell anyway. In A Golden Crown — Confesses everything except the crime — and walks free. In The Pointy End — Talks the hill tribes into an army and marches with his father. In Baelor — Wakes up after his own battle, victorious and unconscious. In Fire and Blood — The son Tywin never wanted, sent to do what no one else can.

Season Two. In The North Remembers — The only Lannister in the city who read a book about ruling. In The Night Lands — Cleans the gold cloaks with a smile and a demotion. In What Is Dead May Never Die — Three secrets, three councillors, one leak. In The Ghost of Harrenhal — Enough wildfire to burn a fleet — or a city. In The Prince of Winterfell — Reads siege histories while the fleet closes. In Blackwater — Saved the city; woke up scarred, demoted, and unthanked.

Season Three. In Valar Dohaeris — Asks for his birthright; is told he is an ill-made, spiteful creature. In Walk of Punishment — Inherits ledgers that say the crown is drowning in debt. In Kissed by Fire — Learns his wedding is a battlefield his father chose. In Second Sons — Threatens to geld his king at his own wedding feast. Worth it. In Mhysa — "The north will never forget." He's the only one who understands that.

Season Four. In The Lion and the Rose — Handed the cup; wearing the chains by nightfall. In Breaker of Chains — In a cell for the one murder in Westeros he didn't do. In The Laws of Gods and Men — "I wish I had enough poison for the whole pack of you." In Mockingbird — Laughs in his cell: fate sent him a Dornishman. In The Mountain and the Viper — His life rode on a man who wouldn't step back. In The Children — "I am your son. I have always been your son." Then the bolt.

Season Five. In The Wars to Come — Drinks his way across the sea toward a reason to live. In Kill the Boy — Quotes poetry through the Doom until the stone men drop. In Unbowed, Unbent, Unbroken — Sold with Jorah to slavers; talks his way to the fighting pits. In The Gift — "I am the gift." The two cleverest exiles in the world finally meet. In Hardhome — Advises a queen he just met to spare the man who kidnapped him.

Season Six. In Book of the Stranger — Buys seven years' peace from the Masters; the freedmen call it betrayal. In No One — The Masters' fleet arrives mid-toast; peace was rented, not bought.

Season Seven. In Eastwatch — Smuggled into his sister's city to sell an armistice.

Season Eight. In A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms — "I think we might live." The fire disagrees quietly. In The Bells — Freed his brother, killed his king's peace, broke his own heart. In The Iron Throne — Hand to the fourth monarch he's served: maybe this one will stick.

In the books

A Game of Thrones. In The Road South — Reads by the fire and gives bastards advice worth keeping. In The Eyrie and the Heart — "I confess... to being a dwarf." The sky cells lose their first tenant. In The Green Fork and the Whispering Wood — Survives his own side's charge; wakes up a hero of the wrong battle. In Fire and Blood — Sent to rule the city his family is busy losing.

A Clash of Kings. In The Comet — The realm's most hated job, done by its most underestimated man. In Kings in Every Corner — Plays the council like a harp with three broken strings. In The March on the City — A chain across the river's mouth: his masterpiece, waiting. In The Battle of the Blackwater — "Those are brave men knocking at our door. Let's go kill them."

A Storm of Swords. In Dracarys — A wife who hates the marriage as much as he does. In The Purple Wedding and the Trial — "I did not kill your son. But I wish I had." In Gold, Ice, and the Hanged Woman — "Wherever whores go." Two bolts, one boat, no goodbye.

A Dance with Dragons. In The Lost Lords — "Where do whores go?" A riddle he asks every league east. In The Griffin and the Rhoyne — Talks a hidden prince into invading Westeros now, without the queen. In The Harpy's Bargain — Sold, escaped, and signed to a sellsword company with ink still wet.
