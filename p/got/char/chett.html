The fuller tale

Their story

Chett is the Night’s Watch with the romance scraped off. A leechman’s son from the fetid fringes of the riverlands, sent to the Wall for a killing over a girl who laughed at him, he found in the order the one comfort it reliably offers the graceless: a soft berth, tending Maester Aemon’s ravens and leeches in the warmth of the rookery. Then Samwell Tarly arrived — fat, wellborn, kindly — and was handed Chett’s post, and Chett was sent to the kennels, and hatred found the vocation it had been waiting for all his life.

The prologue of the third book lives inside that hatred, on the freezing summit of the Fist of the First Men. Chett has done his sums: the Great Ranging is doomed, the officers are fools, and he has quietly assembled more than a dozen like-minded brothers into a plan — kill the Old Bear and the officers in the night, scatter, and live soft off a wildling village he knows. His inventory of grudges, chief among them the boy who took his leeches and the fat lordling who took his warm fire, is the Watch’s recruitment ledger read from the bottom: not every man the realm throws away was thrown away unjustly.

He matters because he shows the mutiny before the mutiny. The knives that eventually turn at Craster’s Keep do not come from nowhere; Chett’s conspiracy on the Fist is the same rot surveyed a book early, interrupted rather than cured. The tale gives its most sour man its most bitterly ironic vantage — he is standing his hated watch, nursing his plan, when the horn blows once, then twice, and then a third time, and every grievance in his ledger is abruptly beside the point.

How it ends for them

(book only) — before this story begins.

On the page. Killed on the Fist of the First Men (ASOS, ch. 1)

The kennelman's plot to murder his officers at the Fist died under a triple horn-blast: the Others came first. The books' third volume opens with his last watch, cold hands closing in through the snow.

His end comes on the Fist of the First Men, the night his mutiny was to begin. The plan is set, the knives are picked — and then the horn sounds three times, the signal no living brother has ever heard, and the Others fall on the Great Ranging out of the storm. The prologue closes with Chett fumbling frozen-fingered at his blade as the snow walks toward him, all his careful spite converted in an instant into the terror of a small man in a very old war; the books leave him among the hundreds who never came down from the Fist. The show, having no Chett, owed him no death — his mutiny simply detonates later, at Craster’s, in other men’s hands.

Where the page and the screen part

Chett never appears in the show at all; his function is divided among the mutineers the screen does name. Rast inherits his whining malice, Karl Tanner his predatory leadership, and the general grievance of the Watch’s worst men — that they were promised survival, not sacrifice — arrives on screen fully formed at Craster’s Keep without the slow fuse the book prologue lights. Show-Sam also keeps a trace of him: the hostility Sam meets from certain brothers over his soft duties is Chett’s grudge with the name filed off. Readers meet him only once, in the third book’s prologue, but his eyes are the ones through which the books stage the horn blasts that announce the Others’ assault on the Fist — one of the saga’s great curtain-raisings.
