Melisandre

House Baratheon

A red priestess of R'hllor from far-off Asshai, beautiful and unsettling, who reads the future in her fires and has attached her prophecies to Stannis Baratheon.

The fuller tale

Melisandre of Asshai arrives in the tale already finished — serene, beautiful, dressed in red down to the great ruby at her throat, and armed with the most dangerous thing in Westeros: certainty. The books let one memory slip through the composure: a slave child called Melony, sold to a red temple, lot seven. Everything else about her — her age, which is far greater than her face; her powers, which are far smaller than her reputation, except when they are terrifyingly larger — she keeps behind the smile of a woman who has read the end of the book and is being patient with everyone still on the early chapters.

Her creed is the old Asshai'i dualism: R'hllor, Lord of Light, against the Great Other whose name is not spoken, one war under all the little wars of men. In her fires she has seen the champion reborn — Azor Ahai, the prince who was promised — and she has matched the prophecy to Stannis Baratheon with the confidence of a scholar and, the books quietly show, the method of one: she reads flames honestly and interprets them like anyone who already knows the answer. On Dragonstone she burns the Seven, draws the lady of the castle and half its knights into the new faith, and makes a king's cause into a crusade.

Her powers, when they come, come terribly. The shadow she births beneath Storm's End does what a hundred thousand swords could not; her leeches name three kings and the story obligingly buries them; poison passes through her at a shared cup's cost to the poisoner. Yet the tale is precise about her limits — the fires show true things and she reads them wrong, an admission she makes in the books' single most humanizing beat: a prayer for a glimpse of the promised prince that returns, over and over, only snow. The scariest person in Stannis's camp is also the story's great study in motivated interpretation.

At the Wall her war finds its real front. The books station her at Castle Black through the fourth and fifth acts — advising a young lord commander who does not trust her and is always a little warmer to her warnings than he means to be, working the glamour that swaps a king's face onto a doomed man, and drifting, reading by reading, toward the suspicion that she has matched her prophecy to the wrong man. The show gives that drift a destination: it is Melisandre who stands over Jon Snow's body and, at Davos's urging and with no faith left in herself, speaks the words that bring the story's dead man back — her one unambiguous miracle, delivered at her lowest ebb.

The seasons after spend her like a coin: exposed and banished for Shireen, wandered south, and returned precisely once more, on the worst night in the world, to light the Dothraki arakhs and fire the trench when every torch had failed — and to remind a small assassin what one says to the god of death. Her promise to Davos and to herself is kept by dawn. Whatever else she was — fraud, fanatic, midwife to horrors — the tale's ledger shows the same entry twice: when the dark was total, the red woman made light.

The page and the screen

The books withhold the show's redemption arc — as of the published pages Melisandre has never left the Wall, never faced the reckoning for a burned princess (who is alive in the books), and never resurrected anyone, though the fifth book leaves her standing next to a body with every rifle loaded. Her book characterization runs interior where the show runs iconic: a point-of-view chapter opens her doubts, her mnemonic tricks, her honest misreadings — powders and glamours candidly inventoried alongside true power. The show also invents her ending's great image, the choker that holds back centuries; the books' ruby glows and glamours but has made no such promise. Book readers know a colder question is still open: what the woman who burns people for a living will decide Jon Snow's body is for.

The show: Ended after the Long Night (S8·E3)

The red priestess lit the Dothraki blades, fired the trench, and gave Arya the words that ended the Long Night: what do we say to the god of death? At dawn she walked out the gate, took off the choker, and let her centuries catch her in the snow.

Her death is show-only, and she chooses every part of it. Having done the last things her long life was apparently for — the flaming arakhs, the lit trench, and the quiet word that sends Arya Stark toward the Night King — Melisandre walks out of Winterfell's gate at dawn with the battle won. She takes off the ruby choker, and the centuries she has carried arrive all at once: the red woman ages to a crone and then to nothing in the space of a hundred yards, folding into the snow with Davos watching — the man who swore to execute her, witnessing instead the sentence she passed on herself. In the published books Melisandre is alive at Castle Black, her greatest workings, for good or ill, still ahead of her.
