How it ends for them

On the screen. Fell at Castle Black (S4·E9)

The Magnar of Thenn, scarred and earless, met Jon Snow in the yard of Castle Black when the wildlings breached the gate. He nearly beat Jon to death before taking a smith's hammer through the skull.

On the page. Fell in the assault on Castle Black (ASOS, ch. 55)

The earless Magnar of Thenn led the climb over the Wall's shoulder and the assault on Castle Black's naked southern face. The Watch's traps met him on the ice: he fell screaming from the Wall he'd beaten.

Both tellings kill him at Castle Black, in the assault he led. On screen he gets a villain’s exit: breaking through the gate with his Thenns, he beats Jon Snow half to death in the yard — swordless, outmatched, spitting — until Jon’s hand closes on a smith’s hammer and drives it up through the Magnar’s skull. The books end him with the Wall itself: leading the escalade against the castle’s southern face, Styr and his climbers meet the Watch’s prepared defenses on the ice, and the great stair collapses in fire and frozen thunder, taking the Magnar down with tons of ice — the mountain lord killed by the only thing in the south taller than his mountains. His people’s story continues without him in the books, wearing Karstark colors.

Their chapters, book by book

Where the page and the screen part

The show reinvented the Thenns wholesale. The books’ Thenns are the least savage of the free folk — bronze-armored, Old Tongue-speaking, so orderly that the Watch later marries their new Magnar, Styr’s son Sigorn, to Alys Karstark, founding a house of Thenn kneelers in the north’s heartland. The screen’s Thenns are scarified, bald, and eat their enemies — cannibalism borrowed from the ice-river clans to give season four a memorable villain, with Styr rebuilt to match as a leering brute. Book-Styr is no monster, merely absolute; his quarrel with Jon is professional, not sadistic. The two versions share a name, a mission and a death at Castle Black, and almost nothing else.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
