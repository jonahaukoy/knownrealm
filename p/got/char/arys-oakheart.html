The fuller tale

Their story

Arys Oakheart is a book-only tragedy in a white cloak: a knight of House Oakheart of Old Oak, raised on the Reach's songs of chivalry, who won a place in Joffrey's Kingsguard and discovered what the office had become. The books give him one distinction the reader is asked to weigh carefully — of all the white knights ordered to beat Sansa Stark, Arys alone protested, and when refusal was exhausted, struck as lightly as the order allowed. It is not heroism; the books are precise about that. It is a decent man discovering the size of his own courage, and the measurement shames him for the rest of his short story.

Dorne undoes him kindly. Sent to Sunspear as Princess Myrcella's sworn shield, Arys is a stranger in a warm country — and Arianne Martell, needing a Kingsguard for her queenmaker plot, sets about him with every weapon she owns. His single point-of-view chapter is the anatomy of a seduction and a self-deception: the vows recited, the vows rationalized, love and treason braided together until the knight persuades himself that crowning Myrcella under Dornish law is somehow the honorable reading of his oath. He is the books' gentlest demonstration of their hardest rule — that a man can be led anywhere, provided each step is small and someone he loves is waiting at the end of it.

How it ends for them

(book only) — before this story begins.

On the page. Cut down in Dorne (AFFC, ch. 22)

The white knight let Arianne Martell talk him into crowning Myrcella, and rode at Areo Hotah's line on the Greenblood when the plot broke. The captain's longaxe took him almost gently — Hotah liked him, and made it quick.

His death is book-only and takes less than a page. When the queenmaker conspiracy is betrayed and Areo Hotah's men close the trap on the Greenblood, Arys — seeing the plot dead, his vows twice-broken, and no version of the morning he can survive with honor — sets spurs and charges the captain's line alone, sword up, against a score of spears. The books are careful to make it neither madness nor accident: it is a chosen end, the last decision left to a man who could no longer live as either a true knight or a false one. Hotah's longaxe meets the charge and takes his head half from his shoulders — quickly, the captain notes, because he had liked the man, and quick was the only kindness left on the river that dawn. Myrcella is maimed in the same chaos; Arianne carries the guilt of both into every chapter she has left.

Where the page and the screen part

The show cut Arys along with the entire queenmaker plot; its Myrcella-in-Dorne arc needed no compromised guardian, and his narrative office — the outsider's eyes on Sunspear — went unfilled. The cut is quietly expensive: his chapter is one of the books' key windows on Arianne, on Dornish law, and on what the Kingsguard's vows have curdled into by the war's middle years, a theme the books route through Arys, Jaime and Barristan as a running argument the show only samples. Old Oak's knight remains, for show watchers, a man who never existed.
