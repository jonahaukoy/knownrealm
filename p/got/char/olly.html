Olly

The Night's Watch

A farm boy taken in at Castle Black after wildlings destroyed his village, who becomes Jon Snow's young steward.

The fuller tale

Olly is the war seen from below the age of enlistment. A smallfolk boy from a village north of the last northern lords’ protection, he is feeding chickens one morning when Styr’s raiders come over the hill; by nightfall he has watched the Thenns butcher his parents and his neighbors, and been left alive on purpose — told to run to Castle Black and tell the crows what is coming. The free folk meant him as a message. The Watch takes him in as a mouth to feed, and he stays as something between a recruit and a foundling, learning the bow he could not draw the day it mattered.

The battle for Castle Black makes him a soldier at perhaps twelve years old — and gives him the arrow that history will remember him for, loosed from the yard into the back of the spearwife aiming at Jon Snow. That the spearwife was Ygritte, and that Jon loved her, is a thing Olly cannot know and would not weigh; from where he stands she was one of the raiders who laughed while his village burned. Afterward Jon makes the boy his steward, as Mormont once made Jon — a kindness with an old design, the Watch turning grief into service.

But the design fails, and the failing is the point of him. Olly watches the man he serves — the commander he half-worships — open the gates to the people who murdered his family, feed them, settle them, and ask the Watch to fight beside them. Every argument about the dead beyond the Wall runs off the boy like rain off slate; he was not orphaned by White Walkers. His face, watching the free folk file through the gate, is the mutiny in miniature long before any officer says the words: some debts do not care what is coming.

The page and the screen

Olly is a show invention with a precise job: to give a face and a comprehensible grief to what the books distribute across an institution. On the page, no boy shoots Ygritte — her arrow is anonymous — and no child stands among Jon’s assassins; the conspiracy is grown men of the Watch’s old guard, stewards and officers led by Bowen Marsh, weeping as they strike for the Watch. The show gathered all that institutional resentment into one small, wounded figure the audience had watched grow up, so that the final knife would come from the last hand Jon — or the viewer — could hate. It worked well enough that Olly became one of the most reviled characters in the adaptation, which is its own tribute: he is the Watch’s grudge given a child’s face, and children are harder to forgive.

The show: Hanged at Castle Black (S6·E3)

The boy whose village Styr's Thenns butchered put the last knife in Jon Snow's heart, and hanged for it beside Thorne when the Lord Commander returned. Jon cut the ropes himself and never spoke of him after.

He ends at the end of a rope, and the show does not flinch from it. Olly delivers the last knife in the mutiny — luring Jon into the yard with a lie about his uncle, then stepping through the ring of officers to drive the final dagger in with tears on his face — and when Jon Snow returns from the dead, the boy hangs beside Thorne and the other conspirators, the youngest neck on the beam. He offers no last words; he just stares at Jon until the box is kicked away. Jon cuts the bodies down himself, and it is Olly’s face — the steward, the orphan, the mutineer, still a child — that ends his time as Lord Commander: he hands Edd his cloak and rides away from the Watch that made them both. There is no Olly in the books to meet any fate.
