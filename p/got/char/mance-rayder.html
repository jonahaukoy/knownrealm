Mance Rayder

The Free Folk

The King-Beyond-the-Wall: once a crow of the Night's Watch, now the man who has done the impossible and united a hundred quarreling wildling peoples under one banner.

The fuller tale

Mance Rayder is the Night’s Watch’s most gifted product and its most instructive failure. Wildling-born, taken as a child and raised at the Wall, he became one of the order’s finest rangers — clever, charming, a fighter, a singer — and then walked away. The books give his desertion the texture of a parable: mauled by a shadowcat, he was healed in a wildling hut by a woman who mended his torn cloak with scarlet silk, and when the Watch made him replace the mended cloak with a plain black one, he understood the bargain of his whole life. The Watch had given him everything except the right to own anything, and he went over the Wall to be free.

What he built there had never been built before. The free folk are called free because no one commands them — dozens of peoples, mutually raiding, speaking different tongues, agreeing on nothing but their contempt for kneelers — and Mance welded them into one host: Thenns and Hornfoots, ice-river cannibals and cave dwellers, giants, wargs, and every raider chief with a grudge. He did it without a crown, a throne, or the power to behead anyone, which he is quick to point out makes him the only king in Westeros who is actually elected daily. His tools were persuasion, patience, a harp, and the one argument that beats ten thousand years of feuding: the dead are coming, and the Wall has only one warm side.

That is the tale’s great irony about Mance: the King-Beyond-the-Wall is not invading, he is evacuating. His host drags families, herds and sledges toward the Wall because he has seen what stirs in the far north — his people are dying and rising in the cold, and every battle he fights against the Watch is a fight to put the Wall between his people and their own dead. The order he once served reads the largest migration in history as the assault it was built to stop, and the two sides bleed each other in front of the actual enemy.

His duel of wits with Jon Snow structures the whole northern arc. Mance knows a planted turncloak when he sees one — he did the walk himself, in the other direction — and takes Jon in anyway, testing him against Ygritte, Tormund and the Magnar, teaching him the free folk from the inside. When the Watch’s trickery is finally spent and Stannis Baratheon’s knights shatter the wildling host beneath the Wall, Mance’s kingship ends the way it began: not with a betrayal by his people, who never stopped following him, but with a southern power deciding what to do with a king who will not kneel.

Mance matters because he is the story’s working demonstration that the Wall’s two sides are one people separated by masonry. Ranger and raider, singer and general, kneeler and free — every division the saga draws in the north, Mance has lived on both sides of, and found both wanting. The free folk who eventually pass the Wall and fight the Long Night beside the Watch walk through a gate Mance Rayder spent his life arguing open, whatever name is on the treaty by then.

The page and the screen

The tellings part company at the pyre, and the divergence is total. The show burns Mance in its fifth season’s first hour and means it; the books burn a glamoured Rattleshirt in his place, Melisandre’s ruby-magic dressing the Lord of Bones in the king’s features while the real Mance — hidden under the bone-lord’s rattling shirt — is quietly kept in service. Book-Mance then rides to Winterfell posing as the bard Abel, six spearwives at his back, to steal Ramsay Bolton’s bride out of the heart of the Bolton stronghold — one of the fifth book’s great covert operations, and it ends with the Pink Letter claiming he sits in a cage wearing a cloak of spearwives’ skins. Whether that letter tells the truth is one of the saga’s liveliest open questions; what is certain is that Mance Rayder is alive in the published books, and the show’s clean, honorable ending is an invention.

The characters differ in flavor as well as fate. Book-Mance is a performer to the bone — he once climbed the Wall in a rebuilt cloak to walk Winterfell’s feast hall as a minstrel and look Robert Baratheon in the eye, purely because he could. The show pares away the theatricality and the harp and gives the essentials to a quieter, grimmer man whose greatness is all in his stillness — the version who tells Jon Snow the freedom to make his own mistakes was all he ever wanted.

The show: Burned before the Wall (S5·E1)

The books: Burned before the Wall — or so it seemed (ADWD, ch. 11)

The King-Beyond-the-Wall refused the knee to Stannis and was bound to a pyre before the whole watching Wall. Jon Snow's arrow found his heart before the fire could claim its due — the last kindness between enemies.

In the show he dies for a word. Stannis offers the captive King-Beyond-the-Wall his life for a bent knee and the free folk’s spears for a kneeler’s pledge, and Mance — courteous, regretful, entirely immovable — explains that the moment he kneels, everything he built is unbuilt. Melisandre gives him to her god before the whole watching Wall: bound to the pyre, he holds his composure until the flames climb, and it is Jon Snow’s arrow, loosed across the yard against every order, that reaches his heart before the fire can finish its work. The wildlings remember the mercy; Stannis, pointedly, remembers the disobedience.

In the books the pyre is a magic trick. The man who burns screaming in Mance’s face and Mance’s chains is Rattleshirt, wrapped in Melisandre’s glamour; the true Mance survives as her hidden asset and is spent on the Winterfell infiltration, last reported — by Ramsay Bolton, hardly a disinterested witness — as a prisoner in a cage. Alive in the published books, his final fate unwritten: of all the divergences between page and screen, Mance’s is the widest still open.
