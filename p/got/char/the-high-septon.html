The mob takes him apart with its hands. On the day Princess Myrcella sails for Dorne, the royal party processes back through a city that has been starving all winter while the court feasts ÔÇö and when the crowd's misery finds its voice, dung and curses turn in a heartbeat to riot. The High Septon, borne along in his litter in full regalia, is the crowd's first and most symbolic harvest: the man who spoke for the gods while the gods' children starved is pulled from his litter and torn limb from limb in the street, his crystal crown vanishing into the same gutters his flock lived in. The books add the grace note that he is literally pulled to pieces ÔÇö the Faith later recovers the crown but not, in any assembled sense, the Septon ÔÇö and both tellings stage the death as the city's verdict rather than any schemer's plot: of all the story's murdered institutions, he is the one killed purely by his own congregation. The riot that consumes him nearly takes Sansa Stark too, and announces the truth the capital's rulers spend the rest of the saga relearning ÔÇö the smallfolk are not scenery.

Their chapters, book by book

Where the page and the screen part

The books run through High Septons faster and more pointedly than the show. The fat one of the riot is succeeded, at Tyrion's brisk arrangement, by a pliable old man raised for his loyalty to the throne ÔÇö and when that successor dies suddenly in the night, the novels drop dark hints that Cersei had him smothered for knowing too much, a murder the show never touches; only then do the Most Devout, pressured by the sparrow movement flooding the city, elect the barefoot radical. The show compresses the succession to a single cut ÔÇö jeweled hypocrite to High Sparrow ÔÇö trading the books' study of institutional decay for velocity. Both tellings agree on the founding irony: the Faith's collapse into revolution is not caused by its enemies but catered by its own princes, and the fat man in crystal is the caterer-in-chief.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
