How it ends for them

On the screen. Ended beyond the Wall (S6·E5)

The last greenseer, a thousand years in a weirwood throne, kept the world's memory beneath the hill. The Night King touched Bran in a vision and followed the mark home — the old man dissolved into the dark with his work half-finished.

The show kills him with a touch. When Bran, unsupervised in a vision, is found by the Night King and marked on the arm, the cave’s protections are voided at a stroke — the dead know where the world’s memory lives, and come for it. The Three-Eyed Raven spends his last hours in the only currency he has: he drags Bran into a final vision of old Winterfell and pours the succession into him mid-dream, teacher and archive at once, while outside the children of the forest and Summer and Hodor buy minutes with their lives. The Night King enters the cave and shears the old man apart at the root-throne; in the vision, Bran watches his teacher dissolve into wind and snow with the work unfinished — the world’s memory handed off mid-sentence, which explains a great deal about its new custodian.

In the published books he has no death: Bloodraven sits in his roots still, Bran’s education barely begun, the Night King who kills him on screen not existing on the page at all. Readers of a certain temperament note that a man who was born in one king’s bed, died to the realm’s knowledge at the Wall, and turned up a century later inside a tree has already outlived three or four endings, and may decline this one too.

Their chapters, book by book

Where the page and the screen part

The books give the figure a name and one of the saga’s richest backstories: he is Brynden Rivers, called Bloodraven — a bastard son of King Aegon the Fourth, albino, one-eyed, sorcerer, spymaster, Hand of the King, the most feared man of his age, who ended a rebellion with treachery at a peace banquet, was sent to the Wall for it, rose to Lord Commander of the Night’s Watch, and vanished on a ranging decades before the tale begins. A thousand eyes and one, the riddle-songs call him; finding that dreaded historical figure grown into a tree is one of the books’ great slow-fuse revelations, and it colors every lesson — Bran’s teacher is not a neutral sage but the most cunning political operator of his century, wearing roots. The show never names him, splits the role between two actors, and invents both his death and the Night King’s personal vendetta; the books’ version has no mark on Bran’s arm, no cave-fall — and is alive in the published books, still in his chair, still teaching.

The tellings also disagree about the bird. In the books he is the three-eyed crow, and his dream-summons are stranger and sparser; the show’s rebranding to raven tidily matches the Westerosi post-bird and quietly drops the books’ implication — still argued over by readers — that the crow of Bran’s dreams and the man in the cave may not be exactly the same thing.
