Their story

Mirri Maz Duur was a godswife of the Lhazareen — the Lamb Men, the shepherd people of the lands southeast of the Dothraki sea, whose meekness is a proverb among the riders who prey on them. She was no village hedge-witch: her mother had been godswife before her, and Mirri had traveled for her learning — herb-craft and birthing-song in her temple, and, the books add, deeper studies besides, including instruction from a maester of the far-off Citadel, Marwyn by name, who had come east hunting the knowledge his order distrusts. She healed the sick of the Lamb Men in the temple of the Great Shepherd, and she was standing in that temple when Khal Drogo’s khalasar arrived to fund a war across the sea.

The sack of her town is the tale’s first unflinching look at what its romances cost. The riders burned the temple, slaughtered the flocks and the shepherds alike, and staked out the women for the khalasar’s use — Mirri among them, a fact she later states with a survivor’s terrible flatness. Daenerys, sickened, claimed her and the other women as her own, and believed — being young, and new to power, and in need of a healer for her wounded husband — that this made her a rescuer. Mirri corrects the arithmetic in one of the saga’s coldest exchanges: saved, she notes, after the burning, after the men, after everything. Look to what her saving was worth.

Her revenge is a masterwork of malice disguised as service. Drogo’s festering wound goes to her poultices; the poultices — or the drawing off of them, the tale leaves the mechanics deliberately murky — go to rot; and when the desperate khaleesi demands the forbidden remedy, Mirri gives her exactly what she asks for: bloodmagic, a life for a life, with the price left unspecified. The rite in the tent takes Rhaego, the unborn stallion who would have mounted the world — monstrous, scaled, long dead by the telling of the midwives — and returns Drogo alive in the sense a stone is alive. Asked when he will be as he was, she delivers the answer the whole story keeps: when the sun rises in the west and sets in the east, when the seas go dry and mountains blow in the wind like leaves. She knew what she was killing, she says plainly. The stallion who mounts the world would have burned cities and broken nations; now he will burn none.

She is the tale’s first true teacher of its deepest rule — only death pays for life; all magic is a transaction with something that keeps accounts — and the fact that she teaches it as an act of war makes her one of the saga’s most morally interesting minor figures. She is a war criminal’s victim committing an atrocity of her own; a healer who weaponized her art; the avenger of a butchered people whose vengeance falls on a fifteen-year-old girl. The story never asks the audience to forgive her, or Daenerys, or anyone in the tent. It asks only that the ledger be read entire.

How it ends for them

On the screen. Bound to a funeral pyre (S1·E10)

The godswife traded Drogo's life for the life of Daenerys's unborn son and called it justice for her burned temple. Daenerys bound her to the khal's funeral pyre, and she sang her death song until the fire took the tune.

On the page. Given to the flames (AGOT, ch. 73)

The godswife repaid the sack of her temple by trading Rhaego's life for a breathing husk of a khal, and told Daenerys the sun would rise in the west before Drogo returned. She was bound to his pyre — and from her death-song came dragons.

Daenerys bound Mirri Maz Duur to Khal Drogo’s funeral pyre, and both tellings are exact about the sequence: the maegi was tied to the pyre with the khal’s body and treasures, the three petrified dragon eggs were set among the wood, and the khaleesi lit the fire herself. Mirri sang as the flames climbed — a death-song, defiant, until the smoke took the tune and the screaming was no longer song. It was not, the tale is careful to establish, mere execution: Daenerys had understood the maegi’s own lesson better than her teacher, and spent her death as a component. Only death pays for life — and three lives were in the fire.

What the pyre returned at dawn is the story’s founding miracle: Daenerys unburnt in the ashes, three dragons at her body, the first in the world in a century and a half. The books’ epilogue to Mirri is the bleak symmetry that her vengeance made her victim into the very thing she died to prevent — she burned one conqueror in the tent, and her burning midwifed a greater one. The dragons that broke Astapor, Yunkai and Meereen — and, in the show’s telling, King’s Landing — hatched in the fire she fed.

Where the page and the screen part

The two tellings track closely; the books simply supply more of everything — Mirri’s education under Marwyn the Mage (a thread that quietly links her to the Citadel’s forbidden shelves), the fuller horror of the sack, and the precise, chilling interview in which she explains to Daenerys that her son’s death was not a mishap but the point. The show compresses her learning into a few lines and shifts some emphases — its Mirri states outright that she intervened to spare the world the stallion’s coming conquests, making the murder overtly political — but keeps her prophecy about Drogo’s return nearly verbatim, which matters, because Daenerys later treats its impossible conditions as a private calendar of grief. Her death is the same in both tellings, and so is what walks out of it.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
