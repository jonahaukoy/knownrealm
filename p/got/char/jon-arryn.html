Their story

The whole tale begins with an old man refusing to commit a murder. Jon Arryn, Lord of the Eyrie and Defender of the Vale, had fostered two boys at his mountain seat — Eddard Stark and Robert Baratheon — and when the Mad King demanded their heads, Jon raised his banners instead. Robert’s Rebellion is named for the man who won its battles, but it was declared by the man who would not hand over his wards; every crown, war and grave that follows descends from that refusal. His house words are As High as Honor, and he is one of the few characters in the story who simply meant them.

He then spent seventeen years as Hand of the King doing the hardest work in Westeros: governing on behalf of a man who would not. Robert hunted, feasted, whored and spent; Jon Arryn balanced, borrowed, soothed and stitched, holding together a peace that existed mostly because he kept renewing it by hand. The price of the rebellion had included his own marriage — Lysa Tully, a girl younger than his memories, wed for Riverrun’s swords — and the match brought him little warmth and a string of griefs before it produced one frail son, born when his father was already old.

His last season of life was spent asking a dangerous question. Something set him digging through the crown’s pedigrees — a fat book of lineages, visits to Robert’s scattered baseborn children, quiet conferences with Stannis Baratheon — and the answer he assembled was the realm’s most explosive fact: the black-haired bastards were Robert’s, and the golden-haired heirs were not. His final reported words, “the seed is strong,” were the finding read aloud with his last breath.

Jon Arryn is the rare character whose importance is entirely posthumous and entirely central. His death is the story’s first domino: it summons Ned south, it puts Lysa’s poisonous letter on the road to Winterfell, it leaves the realm without its one honest broker at the exact moment the truth he found begins to surface. The tale opens with his funeral rites already said — and then spends years teaching everyone, too late, what the old man had been holding up.

How it ends for them

On the screen. A sudden fever (S1·E1)

The Hand of the King died suddenly of a burning fever, quick as a candle snuffed — and half the realm's troubles begin with that bed. He spent his last strength whispering 'the seed is strong,' having learned a truth about the royal children that could not be allowed to live.

On the page. A sudden fever (AGOT, ch. 1)

The Hand of the King burned out in days, a strong man eaten hollow by a sudden fever, whispering 'the seed is strong.' The tale opens with his death — and with the lie Lysa wrote to Winterfell about who arranged it.

He died fast for a healthy man — a burning fever that ate him in days, in both tellings, with his wife at his bedside and his last strength spent on four words about seed. The realm was told it was natural. Winterfell was told, by Lysa’s smuggled letter, that it was the Lannisters. Both were lies, and the second was the more effective: Ned Stark rode south to catch his friend’s killers and walked into the machine that killed him.

The truth surfaces at the Moon Door years later, in the show and the books alike: Lysa herself poisoned her husband with the Tears of Lys, at the urging of Petyr Baelish, who then dictated the letter blaming the Lannisters. Jon Arryn was murdered by his own wife for the love of a man who wanted a war — not because of what he knew, though what he knew was fatal, but as the opening move of Littlefinger’s long game. The books add Pycelle’s cold assist, withholding care once the fever took hold. The most honorable man of his generation died of other people’s ambitions, three separate ways at once.

Where the page and the screen part

Both tellings agree on the essentials; the books simply furnish the investigation. There Jon Arryn works in tandem with Stannis, and the trail is strewn with corroborating detail the show trims: the suspiciously new-made knight Ser Hugh of the Vale, his former squire, who dies conveniently on the Mountain’s lance at the Hand’s own memorial tourney; and Grand Maester Pycelle’s later confession that he deliberately let the Hand’s sickness run its course, sending away the healer who might have saved him, because a dying man’s secrets die with him. The show gives you the corpse in the first hour; the books give you the autopsy across four volumes.
