Edd Tollett

The Night's Watch

Dolorous Edd: a steward of the Night's Watch with a gift for grim humor and expecting the worst of everything, usually correctly.

The fuller tale

Eddison Tollett — Dolorous Edd, and never was a name better earned — is a poor relation of a minor Vale house who brought to the Night’s Watch the one weapon it never runs short of use for: pessimism of the very highest quality. Every duty is a doom, every meal a disappointment, every morning a personal affront, and he narrates all of it in a flat, funereal deadpan that becomes the Wall’s unofficial anthem. The joke, made carefully and at length across the whole tale, is that the Watch’s gloomiest man is among its most reliable.

He serves as squire and steward through the worst of it — the Great Ranging, the horror on the Fist, the mutiny at Craster’s — grumbling steadily through events that break stronger-seeming men. That is the design of the character: Edd’s complaints are a coping mechanism wearing a black cloak, and the men around him lean on it. When Jon Snow rises, Edd rises alongside as his steward and then his friend, one of the few brothers whose loyalty never wavers and never needs stating.

His finest hours are rescues. It is Edd who rides for the free folk when Jon needs the Wall’s enemies saved from a worse one, Edd who holds Castle Black steady in the ugly days after the mutiny against Jon, and Edd who accepts — with visible dismay, as though handed a chamber pot — the Lord Commandership itself when Jon lays down his cloak. The Watch’s last commander of the old dispensation is a man who never wanted anything, which the story quietly suggests is the only safe qualification for power.

Edd’s meaning is proportion. In a saga of kings and prophecies he represents the enlisted man’s-eye view: the cold is cold, the food is bad, the officers are mad, and the work gets done anyway. His humor is the sound of endurance, and the Watch he ends up leading — smaller, stranger, full of wildlings he once fought — survives partly because its commander long ago stopped expecting anything of the world except the worst, and showing up regardless.

The page and the screen

Book-Edd is alive and complaining as the published story stands — Jon posts him to command at Long Barrow, garrisoned with spearwives, a billet Edd accepts with predictable despair — and he has no death to his name on the page. The show promoted him further than the books yet have, to acting Lord Commander after Jon’s departure, and then spent him in the Long Night. His humor translates almost untouched between tellings; several of the screen’s best Edd lines are book lines with the serial numbers rubbed off, which is its own kind of fidelity.

The show: Fell in the Long Night (S8·E3)

Dolorous Edd, last Lord Commander of the old Night's Watch, died in the Long Night doing exactly what he'd always grumbled he would — saving Samwell Tarly. A wight's blade took him from behind; he rose with the dead before the battle was old.

He dies in the Long Night at Winterfell doing precisely the thing he would have grumbled hardest about: saving Samwell Tarly. In the crush of the dead inside the walls, Edd hauls Sam up from under a pile of wights, turns to cover him — and takes a blade through the back, dying on his feet with his usual expression of weary vindication barely disturbed. He rises with the dead before the night is out and is put down with the rest when the Night King falls, then laid on the pyres with the honored dead of the living. He is alive in the published books.
