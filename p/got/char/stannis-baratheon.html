Stannis Baratheon

House Baratheon

Robert's dour middle brother, Lord of Dragonstone: humorless, rigid, and unbending in his sense of what is owed him by right and law.

The fuller tale

Stannis is the middle brother, and the word explains him: born after the charm and before the sweetness, raised in Robert's shadow and Renly's sunshine, with nothing to call his own but rectitude. As a boy he watched his parents' ship break apart on the rocks below Storm's End, and something in him concluded that the gods were either absent or not worth knowing. What he put in their place was law — owed, exact, and without appeal. He does not forgive debts in either direction; the same iron that makes him honest makes him impossible to love, and no one knows this better than Stannis.

His legend was made hungry. During Robert's Rebellion he held Storm's End through a year of siege, eating rats and boot leather while the Tyrell host feasted in sight of the walls, and was saved from surrender by a smuggler who slipped through the cordon with a hold full of onions. Stannis knighted the smuggler and took the ends of his fingers, one act balancing the other — the whole man in a single sentence of justice. His reward for the war was the realm's great insult: Robert gave ancestral Storm's End to Renly and handed Stannis the grim rock of Dragonstone, a slight he chewed on for fifteen years the way he grinds his teeth — audibly, and without pause.

It was Stannis, serving as master of ships, who followed Jon Arryn's investigations to their conclusion and understood what Cersei's children were. When Arryn died of it, Stannis withdrew to Dragonstone and did the arithmetic of succession: with Robert's heirs bastard-born, the crown was his by right — not wanted, he insists, but owed, which to Stannis is the stronger claim. The letters he sends the realm convince almost no one; his brother crowns himself with a hundred thousand swords and Stannis musters five thousand sour lords who mostly follow the red woman now standing at his shoulder. Melisandre offers him what the law cannot: a god who has chosen him personally, and a fire that agrees.

The Blackwater is the hinge of his war and his soul. He comes within a wall's height of taking King's Landing — first through the breach in the books, grimly ashore in the show — and loses everything to wildfire and a Tyrell-Lannister hammer out of the dawn. What follows is the strangest and best movement of his story: alone of all the throne's claimants, Stannis reads the Night's Watch's plea for help, and the would-be king of Westeros sails the wrong way, north, to fight for people who cannot crown him. His horse tramples Mance Rayder's host at the Wall, and for one season the realm's most joyless man is its only functioning king.

The northern campaign is where the tellings part ways in spirit. The show marches him down into desperation, bad snows, and the worst bargain in the story. The books' Stannis is a sharper commander and a colder gambler — taking Deepwood Motte with mountain clans, winning the northern hill lords by fighting for the Stark girl rather than demanding knees, and digging in at a frozen crofters' village to offer the Boltons a battle on ground of his choosing. Around him the books build the tale's most detailed argument about kingship as a job: Stannis listening, grinding, learning — a man with no gift for being loved slowly earning something rarer, which is being followed.

His meaning in the larger tale is the question he asks by existing: what is justice worth without warmth, and what is a rightful claim worth to a realm that needs saving rather than ruling? Davos is his answer and his conscience — the one man who tells him the truth at full price — and the tug-of-war between the Onion Knight and the red priestess for Stannis's soul is the story's most explicit staging of that oldest argument. He is the king the realm deserved and could not want; even his enemies, given a quiet moment, tend to admit both halves.

The page and the screen

No principal character divides book readers and show watchers more sharply. The books' Stannis — dry, precise, bleakly funny in a way he himself never notices — is a serious contender for the best military mind in the story, and his Dance with Dragons campaign is a study in adaptation and grit. The show compresses him toward his worst hour: the humor is pared away, the northern strategy shrinks to a doomed slog, and choices the books frame as Melisandre's temptations become his own decisions, made onscreen with open eyes. Book partisans call the show's version character assassination; the show's defenders point out that the books load the same gun — the tellings disagree about whether Stannis fires it.

Smaller divergences ripple outward. Book Stannis never burns men for apostasy at Dragonstone quite as the show stages it; his brother's shadow is dealt with at Storm's End in front of witnesses the show trims; Renly's peach, Proudwing the hawk, and the bleak wedding-night arithmetic with Selyse are all book textures the show never touches. And the show's Stannis loses his campaign before Winterfell entirely, while the books leave him mid-move on the board — with a letter claiming his defeat that the fifth book pointedly declines to confirm.

The show: Fell at Winterfell (S5·E10)

The last of his army broken before Winterfell's walls, his daughter's ashes behind him, Stannis was found wounded against a tree by Brienne of Tarth. She named her charge — the murder of Renly — and he answered 'Do your duty,' and she did.

In the show, the end comes as a ledger being balanced. His sacrifice of Shireen buys a thaw and costs him half his army by desertion and his wife by her own hand; Melisandre abandons him last. He marches the remnant on Winterfell anyway — because the alternative is that the price was paid for nothing — and the Bolton host breaks it in the field. Wounded against a tree in the snow, he is found by Brienne of Tarth, who has waited years to name the charge: the murder of Renly, by blood magic and a shadow. Stannis does not dispute it. 'Do your duty,' he tells her, and she does. The camera looks away at the last — a famous ambiguity the show later closes by confirming his death.

In the published books Stannis is alive — entrenched in the snows outside Winterfell, planning battle, with Shireen unburned at Castle Black and Melisandre far from his side. A letter reaching Jon Snow claims Stannis is dead and broken; the letter's author is Ramsay Bolton, and nothing in it is verified. The books' readers hold their breath on a battle not yet fought.
