The fuller tale

Their story

The priest who governs the House of Black and White has no name — of course he has no name — and the novices call him the kindly man, because his manner is exactly that: a stooped, cowled elder with a warm voice and a lined, friendly face, who receives the temple’s visitors, dispenses its mercies, and asks its one intolerable question. Each morning he greets the Stark girl hiding in his temple with it — who are you? — and each morning her answer, no one, is weighed and found a lie. He is never angry about the lying. He simply knows, with a professional’s certainty, that a girl who keeps a sword named Needle hidden in the rocks by the harbor has not yet paid the tuition.

His kindliness is the temple’s deepest lesson, and its most unnerving. This is the man who administers a house where the desperate come to drink death from a black pool, where the faces of the dead are harvested and worn, whose order was born in the volcanic slave-mines of old Valyria — the first Faceless Man having understood, as the kindly man serenely recounts, that the god who answers slaves’ prayers for death might be served rather than merely petitioned. He explains the theology of murder the way a maester explains the seasons, tests Arya with lies, sends her to work gutting fish and telling fortunes in the city, takes her eyes and returns them — and through all of it maintains the gentle, terrible patience of an institution that has watched empires end and considers all of them, on the whole, customers.

In the architecture of the books he is Arya’s true examiner: the keeper of the question her whole arc must answer. He offers her, repeatedly and honestly, the door — the temple does not conscript; a girl may leave with her name and her sword and her list — and the offer is the point. The House of Black and White is the only power in the saga that wants nothing from her except everything, freely given. That she keeps choosing to stay, and keeps failing to surrender, is the story; the kindly man is its patient, smiling scale.

How it ends for them

The chronicle records no death for this soul — they are living when the tale leaves them, or their ending is a page the maesters have yet to turn.

Their chapters, book by book

Where the page and the screen part

He does not exist in the show. His role, his temple, and most of his best lines were folded into Jaqen H’ghar, whose familiar face gave the screen version of the House of Black and White a recognizable master — a defensible economy that nonetheless cost the story its eeriest teacher and collapsed the books’ distinction between the priest who tests Arya and the assassin who owes her. Readers of the novels also carry a suspicion the show never needed: that a man who changes faces professionally and takes an unusual interest in a Stark girl’s education may be more entangled in the wider game — the Citadel, the coin, the order’s purposes — than his kindliness admits. He is alive in the published books, asking his question.
