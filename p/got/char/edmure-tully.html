Edmure Tully

House Tully

Hoster's son and heir, brave and warm-hearted and forever a little out of his depth, beloved by the smallfolk of the Riverlands he means to protect.

The fuller tale

Edmure Tully has the misfortune to be a decent man in a story that keeps mistaking decency for weakness. Hoster’s only son, heir to Riverrun, he grows up between a formidable father and two formidable sisters, warm-hearted, brave enough, and forever half a step behind events. What distinguishes him from nearly every other lord in the tale is where his instincts point: when the war comes to the Trident, Edmure opens his lands and his gates to the smallfolk fleeing it. Other lords count spears; Edmure counts mouths, and considers them his.

His war record is the tale’s cruelest joke about good intentions. Charged with holding Riverrun while Robb strikes into the west, he sees the Mountain’s raiders coming, fights them off at the fords — the show stages it as the Battle of the Stone Mill — and returns proud of the first Tully victory of the war. Robb’s face tells him the truth: he was supposed to lose ground, not hold it. The king had been baiting Tywin west into a trap, and Edmure’s gallant, bloody, successful defense sprang it early and saved the man who would sign his family’s death warrant. He did everything right, in the only battle where right was wrong.

The marriage that follows is his apology and his family’s ransom. Robb broke his own pact with the Freys, so Edmure is handed the bill: wed a girl he has never seen, at the Twins, to mend the alliance. Roslin turns out to be lovely and kind — Lord Walder’s one genuine mercy, deployed as anesthetic — and Edmure is escorted to the bedding while the Rains of Castamere start up in the hall. He survives the Red Wedding precisely because he was its pretext: a groom is worth more alive, as a hostage, than dead.

The years in a Frey cell hollow him into the tale’s portrait of survivor’s shame. His son is born to a father in chains; his one appearance of consequence comes at the second siege of Riverrun, where Jaime Lannister quietly explains what will happen to everyone Edmure loves unless the castle opens its gates. Edmure walks his uncle’s drawbridge alone and orders the surrender — the correct choice, the merciful choice, and the one that costs the Blackfish everything — and the story declines to tell us whether to forgive him, because he has never once forgiven himself.

He gets one last scene, and it is entirely Edmure: at the great council in the Dragonpit he rises, smooths his coat, and begins a speech offering himself for the throne — experience, statecraft, the wisdom of hard years — until his niece Sansa tells him, kindly, to sit. He sits. The lords choose someone else. He goes home to Riverrun, to Roslin and his son, alive at the end of a story that killed nearly everyone stronger, prouder and cleverer than he is. There is a moral in that, and Edmure — give him this — would be the first to laugh at it.

The page and the screen

The books are somewhat kinder to his generalship and considerably harder on his ending. Book-Edmure’s battle of the fords is a genuine feat of arms — Tywin’s host thrown back at every crossing — undone only by the trap nobody told him about; several readers have noted that if Robb had shared his plans with his own castellan, the war might have gone otherwise. After yielding Riverrun in the books, Edmure is not freed but transferred: he is last seen being carted off toward Casterly Rock, a permanent guest of the Lannisters, with Jeyne Westerling traveling under the same guard. No Dragonpit speech awaits him in the published books — just the long road west, and a Frey wife and son still at the Twins.
