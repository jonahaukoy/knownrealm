How it ends for them

Died in the Sack of King's Landing, before the story — before this story begins.

Elia died in the Sack of King’s Landing when Tywin Lannister’s soldiers stormed the Red Keep, and both tellings treat the manner of it as one of the war’s defining crimes. Ser Gregor Clegane came to her chambers already bloody: in the books he had dashed the infant Aegon’s head against a wall before her eyes, and then raped and murdered Elia with her son’s blood still on his hands. The show has Oberyn recite the same three verbs like a litany — raped, murdered, killed — and stakes his life on making the Mountain confess to them, which, in the tale’s cruelest irony, Gregor finally does, roaring the confession while he crushes Oberyn’s skull.

Tywin later insists he never ordered Elia harmed — the children were policy, he claims, and Elia’s death was Gregor’s excess, unpunished because punishing it would have meant acknowledging it. Dorne was offered silence instead of justice, and the tale spends decades showing the interest that debt accrues: Oberyn’s death collecting it, Doran’s slow plots built on it, and a mad king’s daughter across the sea who eventually hears the name Elia Martell as one more reason the old order deserved to burn.

Where the page and the screen part

Elia is remembered history in both tellings, and the show preserves her memory almost entirely through Oberyn — his bathhouse monologue, his courtroom fury, and the duel in which he dies demanding her name make her one of the most vivid characters never to appear on screen. The books distribute her more widely: through Doran’s long-game vengeance and the marriage pact that sends Quentyn east, through Arianne’s memories, and through the claim — absent from the show entirely — that her son Aegon was swapped for another infant and lives, which would make the Sack’s most famous atrocity partly a deception. The books also leave deliberately open what Elia knew or felt about Rhaegar and Lyanna, a silence Martin has kept against all fan pressure.
