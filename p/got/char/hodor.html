How it ends for them

On the screen. Held a door (S6·E5)

Wylis the stableboy became Hodor in a single terrible moment threaded backward through time: Bran warged him in the past and present at once, and the boy heard his own death as a command. He held the door. He held it to the end.

Hodor dies holding a door. When the Night King's assault breaches the three-eyed raven's cave, Meera drags Bran's sled for the rear passage while Bran — mind still deep in a vision of Winterfell's past — wargs Hodor to carry them out. At the back door, with the dead clawing through, Meera screams the only plan there is: hold the door. And the command travels. In the vision, young Wylis collapses in Winterfell's courtyard, seizing, hearing his own death decades early — hold the door, hold the door, Hodor — the words grinding down into the one he will say for the rest of his life. Every 'Hodor' ever spoken was the echo of this moment arriving. He holds the door until the dead have torn him apart, and the sled gets away. In the books he is still alive, still hodoring, the door as yet unheld — readers know only that the word is a fuse, not when it burns down.

Where the page and the screen part

The books' Hodor is Walder, not Wylis, and the origin of his condition remains unexplained on the page — the great door-holding reveal, with its time-looped seizure threading a future command back through a boy's whole life, exists only in the show, though the showrunners have said the destination came from the author himself. How the books arrive there, if they do, may differ in mechanism: book Bran's warging of Hodor is more extensive and more explicitly troubling, and Hodor is still alive in the published volumes, sheltering in the greenseer's cave. The show also gave him one flourish the books never did — a glimpse of young Wylis at Winterfell in Bran's visions, a talkative boy with his whole voice still ahead of him.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
