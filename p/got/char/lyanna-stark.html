Lyanna Stark

House Stark

Ned's beautiful, headstrong sister, betrothed to Robert Baratheon. Her abduction lit Robert's Rebellion; she died before the tale, and Ned will not speak of how.

The fuller tale

The whole war fits inside one question about Lyanna Stark: was she taken, or did she run? Lord Rickard's only daughter had the wolf blood her brother Brandon had — Ned's phrase for both of them — and the tale's memories of her agree on the essentials: beautiful in a wild, unfinished way, half a horse herself in the saddle, willful past any septa's mending. Robert Baratheon, betrothed to her, loved a picture of her; her own recorded opinion of the match — that love would never keep Robert from other beds — is the shrewdest sentence anyone says in the whole backstory.

The books hang their richest riddle on her at the tourney of Harrenhal: a mystery knight in mismatched armor, a shield painted with a laughing weirwood tree, punishing three houses whose squires had bullied a crannogman — and a furious king demanding the knight's unmasking. The story, told by Meera and Jojen to Bran as an old tale, never names the Knight of the Laughing Tree; readers have voted overwhelmingly for Lyanna, and the tourney ends with Rhaegar Targaryen riding past his own wife to crown Lyanna Stark queen of love and beauty with a garland of winter roses — the moment, as the books put it, when all the smiles died.

What happened next is the tale's contested ground. The realm's official version — abduction and rape by the crown prince — launched a rebellion, killed her father and brother, and put her betrothed on a throne. The truth the story slowly uncovers is quieter and sadder: a marriage, a tower in the Dornish mountains her prince named Joy, and a girl of sixteen who could not have known her choice would be paid for by everyone she loved. She is the tale's original demonstration that the songs get everything wrong — Robert went to his grave loving a woman who had run from him, and hating a man she may have loved.

Her long shadow is the story itself. The promise she extracted from Ned — promise me, Ned, the words that bloom through his every fever-dream — built the lie at the tale's center; her statue in the crypts is where Robert begins the story and where Jon's truth is finally spoken; and the winter rose she is never seen without in memory becomes the saga's shorthand for everything lost to love and war both. Few characters who never draw living breath on the page shape so much of what breathes around them.

The page and the screen

The books keep Lyanna entirely in memory — Ned's dreams, Robert's grief, the Reeds' Harrenhal story — and have never confirmed on the page what the show staged outright: the tower of joy reveal, the bed of blood, the whispered name of her son. Show viewers know R+L=J as fact, annulment, wedding and all; book readers hold the same conclusion as a near-certainty built from dreams and blue roses, technically still awaiting the author's word. The show also simplified her marriage question — its Rhaegar had his first marriage annulled, a legal tidiness the books nowhere suggest and which Dornish law arguably would not need, since Targaryens had taken two wives before. And the Knight of the Laughing Tree, her best book moment, never made it to screen at all.

Before the tale: Died before the story

Lyanna died at the tower of joy in the Red Mountains of Dorne, at the war's very end, in what Ned's memories call a bed of blood — childbed fever, the cost of delivering the son the tale will spend decades hiding. Ned fought his way to her past three Kingsguard who died keeping the door, and found his sister dying in a room that smelled of blood and roses; her last strength went into a promise — that Ned protect her boy, whose Targaryen name would be a death sentence in Robert's new realm. He kept it by dishonoring himself in the world's eyes, and she went home to the crypts of Winterfell, a northern burial she had asked for over a southern one. The show films the scene twice over through Bran's visions; the books give it only in fragments of fever-dream — petals, promises, a fear of storms — and readers treasure the gaps.
