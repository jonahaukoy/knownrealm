Jaqen H'ghar

The Faceless Men

A mysterious, red-and-white-haired prisoner with courtly manners and unsettling eyes, who speaks of himself as 'a man' and pays his debts in the coin of death.

The fuller tale

A man is introduced in a cage. Among the sweepings of the dungeons that Yoren carts north for the Wall — alongside the brutes Rorge and Biter, who are exactly what they look like — rides a courteous prisoner with red-and-white hair and a habit of speaking of himself in the third person, as though the first were a garment he had misplaced. He is called Jaqen H’ghar, of Lorath, and nothing about him adds up: the manners of a courtier, the calm of a priest, the address of a man for whom the cage is a temporary administrative error. When the column burns on the kingsroad, a girl called Arry throws an axe into the wagon rather than let chained men roast, and the tale’s strangest debt is contracted.

The repayment defines him. At Harrenhal he finds Arya and explains the theology of the thing: three lives were stolen from the Red God at the burning wagon, and three must be returned — she has only to speak the names. The deaths that follow arrive like weather, untraceable and exact, and the sequence ends with the tale’s best bluff: Arya names Jaqen himself, and unnames him only for a price — his help in breaking the northern prisoners free. A man keeps the bargain. Then, work complete, he does the thing that reframes every scene he has been in: he passes a hand across his face and is someone else entirely, and the courteous prisoner is revealed as a Faceless Man of Braavos — a priest-assassin of the Many-Faced God, wearing the world’s faces like weather.

The iron coin he leaves with Arya — and the phrase that activates it, valar morghulis — is the tale’s slowest-burning plot device: a small dark promise that carries her, seasons later, across the narrow sea to the House of Black and White. There the show reunites them, or seems to; the man who greets her wears Jaqen’s face while insisting that no one is Jaqen, that the face is merely a courtesy for a girl who came looking for one. In the temple’s long game of identity — the Waif’s cruelties, the hall of faces, the lying game — he serves as Arya’s master and examiner both, administering a training whose stated tuition is everything she is.

What makes the character more than a hooded mystery is the paradox the tale builds into him: the Faceless Men preach the erasure of the self, and yet everything memorable about Jaqen — the wry courtesy, the fondness he plainly cannot admit to, the precision with which he honors debts — is personality leaking through the doctrine. He gives Arya the gift the temple never intended: its skills without its surrender. When she finally stands before him having killed the Waif and reclaimed her name, his response is not wrath but something dangerously close to a teacher’s pride — finally, a girl is no one — and he lets Arya Stark walk out of the church of erasure carrying its treasury of faces in her hands.

Whoever he is — and the tale takes care that the question has no answer — Jaqen functions as the story’s emissary from its deepest theme: that identity is a mask, a tool, a debt, and a choice. Every major player in the saga performs a self; Jaqen is simply the professional.

The page and the screen

The show’s Jaqen is really two book characters wearing one face. The Harrenhal arc tracks the novels closely — cage, debt, three deaths, the coin — though the names Arya spends differ (the books’ victims are Harrenhal functionaries Chiswyck and Weese, and the third name buys the castle’s fall rather than a jailbreak). But the serene master who trains Arya in Braavos is, on the page, a different figure entirely: the Kindly Man, an elderly priest of the House of Black and White whom the show simply replaced with Jaqen’s familiar face — a sensible economy that nonetheless erases one of the books’ eeriest characters and flattens the temple’s hierarchy. Book Jaqen, meanwhile, vanishes after Harrenhal into one of the novels’ favorite puzzles: a man with his description surfaces in the Citadel at Oldtown — the alchemist who buys a novice’s key with a gold dragon in the prologue of A Feast for Crows — apparently hunting something in the maesters’ vaults, purpose unknown. He is alive in both tellings, insofar as the verb applies to a man who may not exist.
