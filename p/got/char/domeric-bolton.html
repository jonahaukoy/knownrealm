Domeric Bolton

House Bolton

Roose Bolton's trueborn son and heir, a quiet, gifted youth and fine harpist and horseman, who died young (book).

The fuller tale

Domeric Bolton exists only in the books, and only in memory — Roose's trueborn son and heir, dead before the tale begins, invoked in his father's flat voice as the measure of everything Ramsay is not. The portrait assembled from Roose's recollections is startling for a Dreadfort product: a quiet, accomplished young man who rode like a centaur, played the high harp beautifully, read books, squired honorably in the Vale under Lord Redfort, and paged for Lady Dustin at Barrowton. Nothing in him, by every account, ran to his house's old cruelties. The flayed man's line produced, for one generation, something gentle — and the family history disposed of it on schedule.

His fatal act was brotherhood. Lonely for a sibling — his sisters had all died in infancy — Domeric learned of his bastard half-brother and rode up the Weeping Water to find him, against his father's pointed silence on the subject. He got months of Ramsay's company. What he got from it, the maesters called a sickness of the bowels; his father, who does not believe in coincidences that convenient, names it plainly in his one extended reminiscence: poison, and the brother who administered it. Domeric's death is why Ramsay is heir, why Roose keeps his bastard close and watched, and why the phrase 'a peaceful land, a quiet people' always carries, underneath, a father's ice-cold accounting of the son he actually wanted.

The page and the screen

The show never speaks Domeric's name — its Bolton succession needs only Roose, Ramsay and the unborn heir, and the backstory of a murdered trueborn brother would have duplicated the arc the show gave Walda's baby. His absence subtly changes Ramsay: book Ramsay is introduced to readers as a proven kinslayer before he ever takes the stage, his menace pre-loaded, while show Ramsay builds his résumé on camera. Book readers also get the richer echo — Roose's warning to Ramsay that Walda's future sons are not to meet Domeric's fate is a sentence with a body already buried under it.

Their fate — if death has found them, it is written here. Open at your peril.

Before the tale: Died before the story

Domeric died before the story opens, of what the Dreadfort's maester recorded as a sickness of the bowels — and what Roose Bolton, telling the story years later to a captive audience of one, calls murder without raising his voice: Ramsay poisoned him, Roose is certain, for standing between a bastard and an inheritance. The death was quiet, deniable and completely effective — every quality Roose prizes, deployed against him — and his response completes the family portrait: he could not prove it, could not spare his last heir, and so promoted the probable poisoner rather than punish him. Domeric's bones lie with his ancestors; his harp, his horses and his hope of a brother are the books' shortest, saddest character study, delivered entirely in past tense.
