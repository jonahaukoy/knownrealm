Their story

Hizdahr zo Loraq is what old money looks like when the revolution wins: a scion of one of Meereen’s most ancient slaving families — the Loraqs, pyramid-proud, their line older than the Harpy on their walls — who greets the dragon queen’s conquest not with a knife but with a proposal of terms. He is smooth, cultured, fabulously connected, and relentlessly reasonable, and he becomes the voice of the city’s dispossessed masters at Daenerys’s court: reopen the fighting pits, honor the traditions, marry the new order to the old, and Meereen can be governed rather than merely occupied. Every empire produces a Hizdahr; the question the tale poses is whether he is the solution to the insurgency or its lobbyist.

His courtship of the queen is politics conducted as patience. In the books he petitions for the pits so many times Daenerys makes his visits a bitter joke — and then, as the Sons of the Harpy bleed her city nightly, he arrives at the real offer: marry him, join Loraq legitimacy to Targaryen power, and the killings will stop. The killings, remarkably, stop — ninety days of peace, exactly as promised, a fact Daenerys files where the reader files it, under evidence of either his good faith or his command of the murderers. The show compresses the same arc into blunter beats: an urbane apologist for tradition, elevated from hostage to consort, whose marriage to the queen is sealed even as she keeps her sellsword lover, and whose great day at the reopened pits goes as badly as any day in the series.

What distinguishes Hizdahr from the tale’s other collaborator figures is that he may simply be right. His case — that peace is bought with symbols, that the pits and the marriage cost less than the graves, that Meereen cannot be ruled by fire against its own culture forever — is the sanest version of the argument Daenerys spends two books and three seasons losing. The tale never quite lets him be trusted (his family’s wealth was slaves; his peace smells of the Harpy) and never quite lets him be dismissed, which makes him one of the Meereen arc’s most useful instruments: the man whose reasonableness forces the queen, and the audience, to price her principles.

His marriage makes him, briefly, King of Meereen — a title he wears with more comfort than his wife wears hers — and the fighting pits’ grand reopening is his masterpiece and his ruin in the same afternoon: the day the old Meereen he restored and the new world his wife made meet in the sand, with locusts in the books and knives in the show, and a black dragon descending on both.

How it ends for them

On the screen. Killed at the fighting pits (imprisoned in the books) (S5·E9)

The nobleman who preached patience and married a queen to buy peace was knifed in the fighting pits by the very Sons of the Harpy he was thought to lead. The peace died in the same hour.

In the show Hizdahr zo Loraq dies at the reopening of the fighting pits of Meereen — the very institution he had spent his whole arc lobbying to restore. When the Sons of the Harpy rise from the crowd in their golden masks and turn Daznak’s Pit into an abattoir, the king-consort is caught in the first rush and stabbed repeatedly, dying on the marble he had argued was the price of peace, killed by the insurgents whose cause he had seemed, to half the court, to represent. The show plays the irony absolutely straight: whatever Hizdahr was — collaborator, conspirator, or the one honest broker in Meereen — the Harpy’s knives did not pause to ask.

In the published books he is alive: surviving the pit, ruling briefly as king while the queen is lost in the Dothraki sea, then arrested by Ser Barristan on suspicion of the locust poisoning and held prisoner as the siege of Meereen begins. His guilt or innocence is one of the Meereenese knot’s genuinely open questions — Martin has left the poisoner unnamed — and his fate, like the city’s, waits on The Winds of Winter.

Where the page and the screen part

The endings part company completely. In the books Hizdahr survives Daznak’s Pit — it is he who urges Daenerys to try the honeyed locusts that fell her champion, a detail that makes him the poisoning’s chief suspect — and after Drogon carries the queen away he rules Meereen in her name until Ser Barristan, convinced of his treachery, deposes and imprisons him in a quiet coup. The published novels leave him alive in custody, his guilt genuinely unresolved: the books offer at least one other credible poisoner, and Hizdahr’s terrified protestations read as plausibly innocent. The show wanted no such ambiguity — its Hizdahr is stabbed to death by the Sons of the Harpy in the pit massacre, dying at the hands of the insurgency he was widely suspected of fronting, a tidy irony the novels deliberately withhold. Book readers also note the pale, elegant detail the show dropped: the peace he delivered was real, whoever paid for it.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
