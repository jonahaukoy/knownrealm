Oberyn Martell

House Martell

The Red Viper: Doran's hot-blooded younger brother — poisoner, duelist, scholar and lover — who has never forgiven the Lannisters for the murder of his sister Elia.

A soul of House Martell, born at Sunspear.

How it ends for them

On the screen. Killed in single combat (S4·E8)

The Red Viper danced circles around the Mountain in Tyrion's trial by combat, demanding a confession for his sister Elia. He got the confession — with Gregor's thumbs in his eyes and his skull bursting in the Mountain's fists.

On the page. Killed in trial by combat (ASOS, ch. 71)

The Red Viper fought the Mountain for Tyrion's life and his sister's memory, poison on his spear and 'say her name' on his lips. He had his confession — Gregor roared it while he crushed the prince's skull with his bare hands.

He dies in the duel he was winning, of the victory he actually wanted. Champion for Tyrion against Gregor Clegane, Oberyn fights the trial by combat entirely on his own terms — a spear against a mountain, wine in one hand in the books' telling, poetry of footwork in the show's — and puts the great knight down, poisoned steel through the armor's seams. Then, with the verdict his for the taking, he declines it: circling the fallen giant, demanding the confession, the name, the order-giver, refusing to end the collection before the debt is read out in full. The Mountain's hand finds his ankle; the rest is the story's most infamous minute — Oberyn borne down, thumbs in his eyes, his skull burst in Gregor's fists even as the dying monster roars out the confession at last. Both tellings agree on every essential, including the bitter accounting: the Viper got his names said, Tyrion got a death sentence, and Gregor got Oberyn's manticore-venom parting gift, which the books make the seed of something worse than death. Dorne's answer to the news fills the rest of both sagas.

Where the page and the screen part

Book and show Oberyn are unusually aligned in character and diverge in aftermath: the books make him the trigger of the entire Dornish master-plot — his death radicalizes Arianne's generation, forces Doran to reveal the Targaryen pact, and leaves eight Sand Snakes (not three) for the prince to disarm — while the show routes his legacy into Ellaria's vengeance coup, a plot his book paramour explicitly argues against. Book texture the show trims: the Yronwood duel that named him, the maester's links, a stint founding a sellsword company, and his correspondence with the lamed Willas Tyrell — the books' sly proof that the Viper's venom was always selective. The brothel diplomacy and the cell-side account of Elia are both tellings' shared jewels, near verbatim.

Their journey

Where the two tellings send them to the same place the road runs whole; where they part, the screen keeps the left and the books the right.
