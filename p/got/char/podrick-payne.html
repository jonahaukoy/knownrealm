Podrick Payne

A shy, tongue-tied young squire of distant Lannister blood, quietly braver and more capable than anyone gives him credit for.

The fuller tale

Podrick Payne is assigned to Tyrion Lannister as an insult and becomes the best decision nobody ever made. A distant, penniless twig of the headsman's branch of House Payne — orphaned young, semi-educated, and afflicted with a stammer that surrenders every sentence halfway — he is foisted on the new acting Hand as squire precisely because no one of standing wanted either of them. Tyrion's initial inventory of the boy finds a mumbling shadow who can barely pour wine; what the inventory misses, everyone misses, which becomes the running joke of Podrick's whole existence. He is the saga's most consistently underestimated person, in a story whose central lesson is what underestimation costs.

The Blackwater converts him from joke to footnote-of-legend in one thrust. When Ser Mandon Moore's sword turns on Tyrion in the burning chaos, it is Pod — twelve-ish, terrified, sworn to nothing but the man in front of him — who kills a knight of the Kingsguard and drags his maimed lord out of the battle. Characteristically, he never mentions it; Tyrion has to reconstruct his own rescue from testimony. The service that follows is the show's gentlest comedy — the brothel visit whose professionals refuse payment, a mystery that pursues Pod for seasons — and its quietest loyalty: at the trial's end, with Tyrion condemned and association lethal, Pod has to be ordered away, and goes only because staying would waste his life on a farewell.

His second education begins where the first ends. Attached to Brienne of Tarth as squire — another pairing of the realm's unwanted — he weathers her initial contempt, learns the sword from her drills and the world from her example, and grows across the seasons into something the early Pod could not have imagined: competent. The show marks the miles in small currency — he holds his own in scraps, manages the diplomacy his blunt lady cannot, and, on the eve of the Long Night, turns out to possess the voice that sings the last night of the world to sleep. By the finale he stands in a white cloak of his own, Ser Podrick of the Kingsguard, wheeling the king he once could not have addressed without stammering. Every institution in the saga fails; Pod is the counter-argument in livery — the person who simply keeps faith, keeps learning, and outlasts the cleverness of everyone who ever laughed at him.

The page and the screen

Book Pod is younger, smaller and still mid-journey. The novels keep his Blackwater heroics and his refusal to abandon condemned masters — he follows Brienne uninvited across the Riverlands because Ser Jaime's lady is his best road back to the lord he lost — but his arc through the published pages ends in genuine peril: taken with Brienne by the Brotherhood Without Banners in its merciless late incarnation, he is under a noose in her final chapters, and his survival hangs on the word Brienne shouts as the rope draws — a cliffhanger the books have never resolved on the page, though the story's grammar and Brienne's later movements imply the boy was cut down in time. The show, having no patience for ambiguity about its most likable squire, carries him clear of the Riverlands entirely — no Brotherhood, no noose — and invents his whole later flowering: the training montages, the song, the white cloak. The famous brothel mystery is the show's too; book Pod's talents remain, as far as the record runs, strictly military.
