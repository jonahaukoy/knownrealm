Catelyn Stark

House StarkHouse Tully

Born a Tully of Riverrun, Lady of Winterfell by marriage: fierce in defense of her children and never quite at peace with the bastard raised beneath her roof.

The fuller tale

Family, duty, honor — Catelyn Tully was raised on her house's words at Riverrun and, unusually for the tale, actually believed them in that order. Hoster Tully's eldest ran her father's castle from girlhood after her mother died, mothered her small sister Lysa and fostered Littlefinger without ever noticing what he was becoming, and was promised to Brandon Stark, heir to Winterfell. The war took Brandon; the alliance stood; and Catelyn married the quiet brother instead — a wedding to one man while grieving another, which is the Tully words compressed into a single ceremony.

The marriage that began as duty became the tale's one solid love story, and its single flaw sat at the family table every day. Ned came home from war with a bastard and no explanation, and Catelyn — who could forgive her husband anything except silence — spent fourteen years being courteous to Jon Snow and never once warm. It is the least flattering thing about her and the most human: the books let her know it, and let her fail to fix it, which is braver writing than making her either villain or saint.

The catspaw's dagger turns her from lady to actor. It is Catelyn who traces the blade to King's Landing, Catelyn whose old friendship with Littlefinger swallows the lie about Tyrion, and Catelyn who — meeting the dwarf by chance at the crossroads inn — calls on her father's bannermen and arrests him at swordpoint. It is the single most consequential impulse in the early tale: the lioness answers, the riverlands burn, and the war everyone was drifting toward arrives on schedule and ahead of it.

In her son's war council she is the voice the young men need and resent — release the Kingslayer's captured father? no; treat with Renly? she goes herself, and watches a shadow murder a king in front of her. Her two great acts of the war are both a mother's: bargaining her son's marriage to Walder Frey's brood to buy a bridge, and freeing Jaime Lannister — treason by any measure — on the slender hope of trading him for her daughters. The first buys the war; the second buys nothing; both are the same decision, made by a woman who never once chose the war over the children it was allegedly for.

Catelyn is the tale's argument that competence and tragedy are compatible. Nearly every read she makes is right — she distrusts the Freys, the Boltons and the Greyjoy boy years before anyone else does, and the story would be a short, happy one if her son had taken her counsel entire. Her failures are all failures of love miscalibrated: too little for the bastard, too much trust in the friend from childhood. She is what the songs never bother to be about, a middle-aged woman being right in rooms full of men being confident.

The page and the screen

Catelyn is one of the books' central point-of-view voices — the entire northern war arrives through her eyes, and her interior monologue of grief, calculation and guilt is among the saga's best sustained writing. The show necessarily flattens that into Michelle Fairley's performance, which critics rightly praised, but it also hands her material the books never wrote: the invented confession about praying for baby Jon's life is the show's, and her marriage's arithmetic shifts once Talisa of Volantis replaces the books' Jeyne Westerling as the queen her son should not have married.

The structural difference is where each telling leaves her weight. In the books she is the reader's window on Robb — he has no chapters of his own — so his kingship, his mistakes and his charm all reach us filtered through a mother's fear. Show viewers meet Robb directly and lose that doubled vision, which quietly makes show-Catelyn seem more peripheral to a war the books insist she co-authored.

The show: Slain at the Red Wedding (S3·E9)

The books: Killed at the Red Wedding (ASOS, ch. 52)

She watched her firstborn murdered under a roof that had sworn them safety, with the Frey wife she'd bargained for standing witness. She opened Lady Frey's throat as her last act, and Black Walder opened hers.

She died at the Red Wedding, under a roof whose bread and salt she had eaten, watching everything she had bargained for turn to slaughter. When the Rains of Castamere began and the quarrels flew, Catelyn's last play was pure Tully: she seized a hostage — Walder Frey's own kin — and offered a trade, her son's life for his. Frey let her cut the man's throat for nothing. She had watched Robb die by then; the Freys opened her throat a moment later. The show gives the hostage as Frey's young wife; the books give her the lackwit Jinglebell, and add that the Freys threw her body naked into the Green Fork as a mockery of Tully funeral rites.

And in the books, the river gives her back. Three days dead, fished from the water, she is granted the kiss of life by Beric Dondarrion — who passes his last resurrection to her and stays dead to pay for it. What rises is Lady Stoneheart: throat-scarred, half-voiced, unforgiving, hanging Freys and fair-weather friends from riverland trees at the head of the Brotherhood without Banners. The show cut her entirely — the single most debated omission of the whole adaptation. Readers are still waiting to learn what the mother's ghost does to the sons' war; viewers were never told she existed.
