How it ends for them

On the page. Beheaded at Baelor's sept (AGOT, ch. 66)

He confessed treason on the steps of Baelor's sept to buy his daughters' lives, as the deal demanded. Joffrey wanted a head instead, and Ser Ilyn took it with Ice while Arya stared at the statue's plinth and Yoren pulled her away.

The deal was straightforward: confess to treason, take the black, and his daughters live. Ned ÔÇö coached by Varys, begged by Sansa ÔÇö spent the last thing he owned and lied to the crowd on the steps of the Great Sept of Baelor, proclaiming Joffrey the true king. Joffrey then discovered the one thing no one had planned for: a king does not have to keep his mother's bargains. He called for Ser Ilyn Payne, and the sword that took Ned Stark's head was Ice ÔÇö his own greatsword ÔÇö while Sansa screamed from the platform and Arya watched from the plinth of Baelor's statue until Yoren of the Night's Watch turned her away from it.

The two tellings differ only in small, sharp details ÔÇö the books give Arya's view from the statue and Yoren's growled 'boy' as he pulls her into the crowd; the show gives Ned's last searching look for his daughter and a fade to sound. The aftermath is identical and enormous: the North rises, the realm splits five ways, and a story that had seemed to be about whether the hero could survive politics announces that it was never that kind of story. His head went on a spike; his bones went north, in time, to the crypts where Starks belong.

Where the page and the screen part

Book Ned is a point-of-view character, and it changes everything: fifteen chapters inside a mind the show can only photograph. Readers get the fever dreams of the tower of joy years before any adaptation could film it, the guilt that flares whenever Jon's mother is mentioned, and the slow horror of a man watching his own investigation close around his neck. Sean Bean's performance builds the same man from the outside ÔÇö wearier, grayer, aged up along with the rest of the adult cast ÔÇö and the show trades the dreams for exposition, which is why viewers met the tower of joy six seasons after readers did.

Smaller divergences accumulate. In the books the fight outside the brothel ends with Ned's leg crushed under his dying horse, not a Lannister spear through the knee, and his last weeks are spent in a poppy haze that makes the black-cell scenes with Varys hit differently. The books also thread rumors around him the show drops entirely ÔÇö half the realm believes Ashara Dayne was Jon's mother, and Ned lets them, because a slander that protects a promise is cheap at the price.
