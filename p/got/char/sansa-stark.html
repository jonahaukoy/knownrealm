Sansa Stark

House Stark

The elder Stark daughter, courteous and dreaming of songs and southern knights, betrothed young to a prince. Her direwolf was named Lady.

The fuller tale

Sansa begins as the realm's best student of a curriculum that turns out to be a lie: songs, courtesy, tourneys and gallant princes. The elder Stark daughter was everything a southern septa could wish — beautiful in the Tully coloring, perfect at needlework, in love with the idea of the court before she ever saw it — and her betrothal to Prince Joffrey seemed to be the songs coming true on schedule. It delivered her into the truth at the worst possible address. The butcher's boy, her wolf Lady dead for a lie, and finally her father's execution — begged for, bargained over, watched from a platform — ended her childhood in a single stroke.

What looks like passivity in the seasons that follow is actually apprenticeship. A hostage of the crown after her father's death, she survives Joffrey's beatings-by-proxy, the mob on the day of the riot, and the exquisite daily terror of a court where her family are traitors — armed with nothing but a perfect curtsy and a memorized smile. 'A girl of porcelain, then ivory, then steel': the books track the hardening from inside her own head, and the show tracks it across Sophie Turner's face, but both agree on the mechanism. Sansa learns every court she is trapped in, and no one teaches her more than the people hurting her.

The middle passage of her story is a graduate course taught by monsters. Cersei tutors her in cynicism between insults; Margaery and the Queen of Thorns show her kindness with an agenda attached; a forced Lannister marriage makes her a Lannister in name to save Winterfell's claim for other people's use; and on the night of Joffrey's wedding, Littlefinger's escape offers rescue at the price of becoming his creature. In the Vale — as Alayne, a bastard-born girl with dyed hair and a dead woman's secrets — she learns the last discipline: how lies are administered, and how a player differs from a piece.

The show then sends her home to the worst classroom of all. Married to Ramsay Bolton in the castle her family built, brutalized behind doors the audience was not spared, she escapes over the walls with Theon Greyjoy's broken help and reaches the one brother left to reach. It is Sansa who talks Jon into fighting for Winterfell, Sansa who writes to the Vale, and Sansa who feeds Littlefinger's knights into the Battle of the Bastards at the exact hour they can decide it. Her testimony at the kennels afterward, delivered without raising her voice, is the coldest sentence passed in the entire tale, and no one who watched her arc calls it undeserved.

The last movement belongs to her. She governs Winterfell through the Long Night's logistics while others swing swords, out-plays Littlefinger to his death in the hall he schemed to own — the sisters he tried to set against each other trying him instead — and, when the wars end, holds the North's independence out of the wreckage while every other crown is melted or buried. Queen in the North, crowned in the castle she survived: the girl who wanted to marry a king, corrected by experience into the person the kings should have been.

Her meaning in the larger tale is the rehabilitation of the skills the story pretends to despise. Sansa never wins a battle, never touches a sword, never rides a dragon; she watches, remembers, endures and administers, and ends holding more than almost anyone who did. In a saga obsessed with the price of power, she is the rare character who pays the price first and collects afterward.

The page and the screen

The deepest divergence in all the Stark children is here: book Sansa has never set foot in Winterfell since the first volume. Her published-books story ends in the Vale, still Alayne Stone, managing Littlefinger's game among the lords declarant and a sickly little lord cousin — the entire Ramsay marriage belongs to the show, which reassigned the books' 'false Arya' plot (poor Jeyne Poole's) to Sansa to keep a beloved actress central. The change remains the adaptation's most argued-over decision: it bought the Battle of the Bastards its emotional engine at the cost of subjecting Sansa to horrors her book counterpart has so far been spared.

The books also age her down — eleven when the story begins — and give her one of the saga's great unreliable-narrator threads: the memory of the Hound's kiss that never happened, growing more real each time she recalls it. Small inventions ripple outward too: her snow-castle scene in the Eyrie, rebuilding Winterfell in miniature, is book-only and beloved; her dyed hair and false name make espionage of chapters the show compressed into a single season of black dresses. Two versions, one direction of travel — but readers genuinely do not know yet whether the books will walk her the show's road home.

In the show, season by season

Season One. In Cripples, Bastards and Broken Things — Dreams of tourneys; gets Ser Hugh's blood instead. In You Win or You Die — A pawn who doesn't know the game has started. In The Pointy End — Made to write the letter naming her father traitor. In Fire and Blood — Stops being a little bird and starts surviving.

Season Two. In Garden of Bones — Learns court courtesy is armor, and armor can be stripped. In The Old Gods and the New — The mob wanted the wolf girl. The Hound wanted otherwise. In Blackwater — Keeps the women singing while the queen drinks truths. In Valar Morghulis — Freed from Joffrey — into Littlefinger's orbit.

Season Three. In Dark Wings, Dark Words — One honest sentence, spoken to the only people who asked. In The Climb — Watches her ship to safety sail without her. In Second Sons — A Lannister cloak on her shoulders she never asked for.

Season Four. In The Lion and the Rose — Free of the lion's den at the worst possible moment. In Breaker of Chains — Saved by the man who profits most from saving her. In First of His Name — Her aunt's welcome curdles between the soup and the sweets. In Mockingbird — Six hundred feet of open air beneath her aunt's grip. In The Mountain and the Viper — Dyes her hair dark and stops waiting to be rescued.

Season Five. In The House of Black and White — Chooses the devil who talks over the stranger who swears. In High Sparrow — Home again, as a bride in the house of her family's butchers. In Kill the Boy — Ramsay's kindness is a knife shown slowly. In Unbowed, Unbent, Unbroken — Unbowed and unbroken will have to wait. She survives. In The Gift — One candle in the broken tower — and the wrong man holding it. In Mother's Mercy — The jump is not the frightening part anymore.

Season Six. In The Red Woman — Says the oath's answer through frozen tears. In Book of the Stranger — "Where will we go?" "Home." In The Door — Made Littlefinger say out loud what he sold her into. In Battle of the Bastards — Won the battle with a letter Jon never asked about.

Season Seven. In The Dragon and the Wolf — "You are guilty, Lord Baelish. How do you answer these charges?"

Season Eight. In The Last of the Starks — Keeps one promise four minutes. The secret starts to move. In The Iron Throne — Queen in the North, crowned in the hall she survived.

In the books

A Game of Thrones. In The Hand Investigates — The tourney is her songs made flesh — for one more month.

A Clash of Kings. In The Kraken in the Godswood — Saved from the mob by the Hound's steel and fury. In The Battle of the Blackwater — The Hound comes to her room smelling of fire and wine, and leaves his cloak. In Valar Morghulis — Discarded by a king — the safest she's been in a year.

A Storm of Swords. In The Purple Wedding and the Trial — Alayne Stone, dark-haired, learning the game from its author. In Gold, Ice, and the Hanged Woman — Watched her aunt fly and learned her keeper's oldest secret: only Cat.

A Feast for Crows. In The Queenmaker — Runs the Eyrie in all but name while her 'father' plays for the Vale.
