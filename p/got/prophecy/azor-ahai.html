Azor Ahai Reborn

The prince that was promised

A promised hero who will be born again amid salt and smoke to wake dragons from stone and stand against the dark.

The prophecy

Unlike most of the visions gathered here, this one was never handed to a single listener on a single night. It has no tent, no fire, no frightened girl. It is a doctrine ÔÇö the central article of faith of the red religion of RÔÇÖhllor, preached in temples from Volantis to Braavos and taught in Asshai by the Shadow, where the priests hold that the world was once swallowed by a darkness a generation long and that one man broke it. That man is named Azor Ahai. The red faith does not merely remember him; it expects him, and it has expected him for a very long time.

The prophecy arrives in Westeros twice over, from two directions, and the two versions do not quite match. Melisandre of Asshai brings the AsshaiÔÇÖi form to Dragonstone in the first year of the story, in the service of a king she has already decided about. The other form is older on Westerosi soil and much quieter: a Valyrian tradition, kept in the Targaryen family and in the scrolls of the Red Keep, concerning a figure usually rendered in the common tongue as the prince that was promised. A woods witch is said to have told the last generations of the dynasty that the promised one would be born of a particular royal line, and at least one Targaryen king is thought to have died attempting to force that birth along. So the same expectation has been sitting in two separate traditions, in two languages, for centuries ÔÇö which is the beginning of most of the trouble.

What the legend actually describes falls into two halves. The first is the forging. Azor Ahai laboured to make a weapon fit for the long dark, and the making cost him three attempts: the first blade, quenched in plain water, shattered; the second, quenched in the living heart of a lion, shattered as well; the third he tempered in his own wife, who loved him and consented, and the steel drank her life and her soul and came out of her alight. That sword is Lightbringer, and in the stories it burns without being consumed and gives warmth as well as light. The moral of the making, if it has one, is that the weapon which saves the world is paid for by killing the thing you most love ÔÇö a detail that ought to alarm anybody hoping to be the hero of it.

The second half is the return, and it is the half people quarrel about, because it is a checklist. When the cold comes again, the legend says, the hero will be born a second time. The signs given are these: a star that bleeds in the sky; a birth or a rebirth attended by both salt and smoke; the waking of dragons out of stone; and a burning sword drawn out of a fire by the one who is meant to hold it. Nothing in the list is dated, none of the four terms is defined, and the tradition supplies no test for telling a true claimant from a hopeful one. It says only that the hero will come, and that the alternative is the dark.

Where it comes from

The red faith is old, foreign and confident, and it is not native to Westeros at all. Its priests read fire, its temples burn night-fires against the dark, and its theology is a straightforward war between one god of flame and life and a nameless adversary of cold and death. In that theology Azor Ahai is not a saint but a weapon ÔÇö the champion the god raised the last time and will raise again. Asshai by the Shadow, the ruined city at the far edge of the known world where Melisandre was trained, keeps the fullest version of the texts, which is not a reassuring provenance: almost nothing that comes out of Asshai is meant kindly.

The same story exists elsewhere under other names. Several eastern peoples remember a hero who fought the same darkness with a different face and a different sword, and the northern version, told in Westeros for thousands of years, remembers a figure called the Last Hero who went into the cold with companions and a blade and came back alone. Whether these are one legend that scattered or several unrelated memories of one winter is exactly the sort of question the maesters enjoy and cannot settle. Maester Aemon, who had more reason than most to care, spent decades treating the Targaryen version as a serious historical problem rather than a devotional one.

The CitadelÔÇÖs general posture towards prophecy is cool. Maesters are trained to be sceptical of magic and of the men who sell it, and the orderÔÇÖs recorded view is that prophecies are unreliable instruments that cut the hand holding them. That scepticism is not the same as disproof. The awkward fact, from the maestersÔÇÖ point of view, is that the red priests have demonstrated genuine power, and that the Targaryens went on believing this particular prophecy right through a period when they had every worldly reason not to bother.

It has been claimed before. Rhaegar Targaryen worked out an interpretation and acted on it. A king before him is widely believed to have died in a fire he lit in pursuit of it. Melisandre announces a claimant within the first years of the story with total assurance. The record of the prophecy, in other words, is not a record of a hero arriving; it is a record of clever people announcing that he has, and being wrong.

How it has been read

MelisandreÔÇÖs reading is the loudest and the most institutionally backed. She holds that Stannis Baratheon is Azor Ahai reborn, and she builds the case out of the checklist: Stannis was on Dragonstone, an island of salt sea and volcanic smoke, when the red comet burned overhead; and at her direction he drew a sword out of a burning sept and carried it away alight. She names that sword Lightbringer. Almost every reader notices what she does not admit ÔÇö that StannisÔÇÖs blade gives light without heat, which is precisely the property the legend insists the true sword has. One widely held view is that Melisandre knows this perfectly well and has produced a counterfeit to shore up a faith she genuinely holds; another is that she has simply mistaken the sign for the thing.

Maester Aemon, working from the Valyrian side and from a lifetime of reading, arrives at a different answer and a crucial correction. The word rendered as prince in the common tongue does not carry gender in High Valyrian; it can as easily mean princess, or simply the promised one. Once that is admitted, Daenerys Targaryen fits the checklist with unnerving neatness. She was born on Dragonstone in a storm of sea-salt; she walked into a funeral pyre amid smoke and came out of it alive; and she woke three dragons out of stone eggs, which is the one item on the list nobody else can even pretend to. AemonÔÇÖs own bitter observation is that he spent his life waiting for the wrong person because he assumed the wrong sex, and had wasted years on Rhaegar before that.

The third serious candidate is Jon Snow, and the case for him is made almost entirely by others rather than by himself. Melisandre eventually turns her attention to him. His circumstances answer the northern Last Hero pattern rather than the AsshaiÔÇÖi one ÔÇö a man who goes past the Wall into the cold with a small company and a Valyrian blade ÔÇö and the questions surrounding his birth give him a claim on the Targaryen strand of the prophecy as well. Against him: he shows no interest in the role whatsoever, and the salt-and-smoke signs have to be worked at fairly hard to fit.

Then there are the readings that treat the checklist as figurative. On this view the bleeding star is the comet and nothing more, salt and smoke are tears and a pyre or a burning town, the stone that dragons wake from is an egg or a wall or a mountain, and the sword is not a sword at all but a person ÔÇö a beloved sacrificed to arm the hero. Read that way, the prophecy stops being a description of an individual and becomes a description of a price. Some readers go further and argue the three heads of the dragon and the promised prince are the same problem stated twice, and that the answer is a company of three rather than one champion.

What can honestly be said is this. The signs are real, in that the events matching them have demonstrably occurred. The identification is not settled, and the books have gone out of their way not to settle it. Every named interpreter within the story ÔÇö a priestess, a maester, a prince ÔÇö has produced a confident answer, and the narrative has taken visible pleasure in showing each of them the flaw in their own reasoning. That pattern is itself the strongest argument for a reading nobody in the story has yet proposed.

The problem with prophecy

This prophecy is a trap in three distinct ways, and it is worth separating them. The first is translation. An entire dynastyÔÇÖs expectations, and a princeÔÇÖs lifeÔÇÖs work, appear to have hung on a grammatical assumption imported by translators into a word that never carried it. That is not a mystical failure; it is a scholarly one, and it is correctable in an afternoon by anybody who bothers.

The second is that the checklist invites forgery. Every one of the signs can be arranged. Salt and smoke can be supplied by any coastal fire; a burning sword can be produced by anyone willing to set a blade alight; a bleeding star will oblige whoever happens to be standing outside when a comet passes. A prophecy which can be staged will be staged, and the story shows it being staged in its first act by someone with an army to legitimise.

The third and worst is the shape of the forging. The legend does not say the hero suffered; it says the hero killed what he loved and that this is what made the weapon work. Anybody who accepts the story as an instruction manual has been handed a theological licence to burn a person they love and call it necessary. The books are quite clear that this licence gets used, and that the people who issue it to themselves are sincere. Whatever else Azor Ahai is, he is the most dangerous idea in the story precisely because he may well also be true.

How it has played out ÔÇö open at your peril

Stannis Baratheon, the announced claimant

MelisandreÔÇÖs candidate was proclaimed early and defended for years. Stannis accepted the title without ever quite believing the religion behind it, treating RÔÇÖhllor as a source of power rather than a matter of faith, and allowed a great deal to be done in his name on the strength of the reading ÔÇö shadow-work against his rivals, burnings on the beach, and eventually the leech and the fire. The one thing he did with the title that reflects well on him was to take his army north to fight the actual enemy while every other claimant in Westeros was fighting over the throne.

The two tellings then part. On the page, StannisÔÇÖs northern campaign is unresolved: the last the reader sees, he is in the snows outside Winterfell, and the only account of what became of him arrives in a letter written by an enemy and cannot be trusted. On the screen his end is unambiguous and squalid. He is persuaded to burn his own daughter Shireen to buy a thaw, his army melts away from him in disgust, he is beaten under the walls of Winterfell, and he is found wounded in a wood by Brienne of Tarth, who kills him for the murder of Renly. Melisandre, watching the flames burn low the night before that battle, sees nothing of what is coming.

The sword called Lightbringer never once behaves like the sword of the legend. It burns and does not warm, and no reader has ever been given a reason to think that was an accident of description.

MelisandreÔÇÖs recantation

The single most important development in this prophecy is that its chief prophet publicly changes her mind. On the page, alone at the Wall, Melisandre admits to herself that she has been reading her own certainties into the fire, that she has seen Jon SnowÔÇÖs face where she expected StannisÔÇÖs, and that she does not in fact know what she is looking at. It is the closest thing to an authorial verdict the books have delivered on the subject.

On screen the same shift happens more brutally: after StannisÔÇÖs defeat she rides north broken, having watched every one of her convictions burn a child for nothing, and afterwards devotes herself to the man she previously dismissed. Her flames were never lying. She was.

Daenerys Targaryen and the dragons out of stone

Daenerys is the only candidate who has actually performed the impossible item on the list. Three dragons came out of stone in a funeral fire in the Dothraki sea, and she walked out of that fire unburnt. Everything downstream in her story ÔÇö the sack of the slave cities, the fleet, the crossing to Westeros ÔÇö rests on that one night, and Maester Aemon, hearing of it late in his life, concluded on the spot that the promised one had been born a girl and that the Watch had spent generations looking the wrong way.

The books have not brought her to Westeros, and her thread remains open at the point the published story stops. The show carried her the whole way, and did so in order to make an argument about her rather than about the prophecy: she reached the Iron Throne, burned a surrendered city and the people in it, and was killed by the man who loved her, in a hall filled with falling ash. Whether that ending vindicates the prophecy or refutes it is genuinely contested. It is worth noting that it reproduces the forging exactly ÔÇö a beloved killed by a heroÔÇÖs hand ÔÇö with the roles reversed.

Jon Snow and the northern pattern

Jon fits the Last Hero shape more than the AsshaiÔÇÖi one, and neither telling ever gives him a burning sword. On the page his thread breaks off mid-sentence: he is attacked by his own brothers at Castle Black at the end of the published books, and nothing about his condition or his parentage has been confirmed on paper by anyone but a boy watching a tree.

The show resolved what the books have not. He was brought back by MelisandreÔÇÖs prayer, retook Winterfell, was revealed as the trueborn son of Rhaegar Targaryen and Lyanna Stark and therefore the rightful heir, and led the war against the dead. He also, in the end, did not defeat the dark ÔÇö he held a line while somebody else did ÔÇö and his reward for killing the woman he loved was exile to the far north. If any single figure in the story has lived out the Azor Ahai forging in its literal terms, it is him, and the story pointedly declines to call him by the name.

Who actually ended the Long Night

On screen the dark was broken by Arya Stark, who had no prophecy attached to her, no burning sword, no salt, no smoke and no dragon ÔÇö only a Valyrian dagger, a great deal of training and the willingness to run at the thing nobody else could reach. MelisandreÔÇÖs last act was to remind her of an old remark about the eyes she would shut, which is the nearest the show came to claiming the fulfilment for the red faith. Many readers regard that as the point being made rather than the point being dodged: the prophecy was real, the dark was real, and every professional interpreter in the story spent decades arguing about the wrong person while the answer grew up in the background.

In the books none of this has happened. The Long Night has not returned in full, no champion has been confirmed, the sword of the legend has not been seen, and the question of who ÔÇö or how many ÔÇö the promised one is remains genuinely open. Readers should be warned that the showÔÇÖs answer was not necessarily the authorÔÇÖs, and that the surviving prophecy threads on the page point in several directions at once.
