The Return of the Long Night

The oldest warning in the North

The oldest warning in Westeros, worn down by repetition into a family motto.

The prophecy

This is the oldest thing in the book and the least like a prophecy. There is no seer, no vision, no fire and no riddle. There is an event that the north insists happened, an expectation that it will happen again, and a set of institutions built at enormous cost by people who believed it enough to spend the rest of their lives on the belief. The prophecy, if it is one, consists of four words a family has been saying for eight thousand years, and of a wall three hundred miles long.

What the stories describe is a winter that did not end. A generation, the tellers say — a night that lasted a lifetime, in which the sun was not seen, crops failed everywhere at once, and mothers smothered their children rather than watch them starve. And out of the cold came something that was not men: figures of ice and pale flesh that killed with weapons of a substance no smith has ever matched, and that raised the people they killed to walk again in their service. The dead did not stay dead. That is the detail every version of the story keeps, from Old Nan telling it to a boy at Winterfell to the fragments the maesters preserve.

The stories say it was ended, and they are vaguer about how. A man went north to look for the children of the forest with companions, horses and a dog, and lost all of them one by one; the tale calls him simply the Last Hero. Somewhere in the telling there is a sword, and there is help from the children, and there is a battle usually named as the one that brought the dawn back. Afterwards the Wall was raised at the edge of the world, the Night’s Watch was founded to hold it, and a family in the north began saying the same short sentence to each other in every generation as a matter of routine.

That sentence is the whole of the prophecy. It carries no date, no sign to watch for, no candidate hero and no instruction beyond the obvious. It does not say the cold will return in a particular year or to a particular king. It says only that this is a world where the seasons run long and without warning, that the last time the dark came it very nearly finished the species, and that the correct posture at all times is to have grain in the cellar and men on the Wall. It is the most useful prophecy in the story and the least dramatic, which is exactly why nobody south of the Neck has thought about it seriously in living memory.

Where it comes from

The source is memory, not revelation, and the memory is oral. The First Men had no writing beyond runes cut in stone, so everything from that period reached the maesters through thousands of years of retelling before anyone wrote it down. This is the Citadel’s main objection and it is a fair one: a story that has passed through several hundred generations of grandmothers is not evidence in any sense a scholar recognises, and the more colourful details — the length of the night, the numbers of the dead — are precisely the details such a process is known to inflate.

The maesters also dispute the arithmetic. The conventional figure of eight thousand years since the Long Night comes from northern tradition and from the Watch’s own count of its Lords Commander, and there are maesters who argue in print that the true span is perhaps half that, and that the histories have simply doubled up on themselves the way long oral records do. That argument is worth knowing about, because it cuts both ways: if the dating is unreliable, so is any comfortable assumption about how long the interval between one Long Night and the next is supposed to be.

The strongest corroboration comes from the far side of the world, and hardly anyone in Westeros has read it. The scholars of Yi Ti keep records of a comparable catastrophe — a long darkness, a general ruin, an accusation of betrayal within a royal house — and other eastern traditions remember heroes who fought a similar dark under names nobody in Westeros would recognise. Two continents that had no contact whatsoever remembering the same winter is a considerably better argument than anything the north can make on its own.

Against all of it stands the seven-pointed star and the Andal faith, which has no Long Night in it at all. The dominant religion of six of the seven kingdoms simply does not carry the story, and the southron lords who take their history from septons have therefore inherited a past in which the thing never happened. That is not scepticism. It is a blank space where the warning ought to be, and it goes a long way towards explaining the response the realm eventually gives it.

How it has been read

For most of the story the dominant reading in the Seven Kingdoms is that it is not a prophecy at all but a nursery tale, and that the creatures in it belong with the other things grandmothers invent to keep children out of the snow. This is the settled opinion of virtually every educated person south of the neck, it is held by men who are not stupid, and it is arrived at honestly: nobody living has seen one, the Watch has not fought one in any recorded lifetime, and eight thousand years is long enough for anything to become decoration.

The northern reading is different in emphasis rather than in content. Northerners do not necessarily believe in the walking dead; what they believe in is winter, which in this world is a real and open-ended catastrophe that arrives without a calendar and can last for years. The words of House Stark are usually read by southerners as a boast about the family’s toughness. They are better read as a household instruction — a reminder to lay in stores, to fix the roof, and not to spend the harvest on a wedding. The prophecy and the housekeeping are the same sentence.

The most interesting interpretive question is what the Wall is actually for. It is far larger than any human enemy requires; the free folk beyond it are not numerous enough or organised enough to justify three hundred miles of ice, and never have been. Something was expected that could not be stopped by a fort and a garrison. There are also persistent indications in the story that the Wall is not merely masonry — that there is something worked into it, and that it holds by more than its own weight. If that is right, then the structure is not a fortification but a ward, and the thing it wards against is the thing the stories describe.

A minority position, and a serious one, holds that the Long Night was a natural event and the monsters are the accretion — that a volcanic winter or some comparable catastrophe produced years without a summer, and that the horrors were added by people trying to explain a sky that had gone dark. It is the reading a maester would give, and it accounts for the eastern records as neatly as any supernatural version does. Its weakness is the same as its strength: it explains the darkness beautifully and has nothing to say about why anybody built the Wall.

What cannot be disputed is the shape of the belief system around it. An entire military order, sworn for life, celibate, landless and forbidden any part in the politics of the realm, exists for one purpose and has existed continuously for thousands of years. Institutions of that severity are not built out of folklore. Something happened. The argument is only about what.

The problem with prophecy

This entry demonstrates a failure mode the others do not touch: a warning can be destroyed by being right for too long. The other prophecies in this collection fail because they are obscure. This one fails because it is clear, correct, and repeated so often that it has become furniture. The Starks say their words at every occasion where words are called for. Southern lords quote them at feasts to be amusing. A sentence that began as an instruction about grain stores has been worn by eight thousand years of repetition into a family slogan and then into a joke, and the wearing-down was done by the accuracy itself.

The institutional decay follows the same curve. The Night’s Watch once numbered many thousands of men in nineteen castles; by the time of the story it holds three, is undermanned by an order of magnitude, and is staffed largely by criminals given the choice between the Wall and a rope. Nobody defunded it deliberately. It simply became impossible to keep taking seriously an enemy that had not arrived in anyone’s great-grandfather’s lifetime, and each generation cut a little more from a budget line that had never yet needed to be spent.

There is no interpretive trap here, no mistranslation and no ambiguity. That is the point. The realm was given the one piece of foreknowledge it could actually have acted on — plain, specific, and repeated by an institution built expressly to keep it in front of them — and the response of the great houses was to spend five years killing each other over which of them should sit in a chair. If the books have a thesis about prophecy, this is it: the problem was never that the warnings were unclear.

How it has played out — open at your peril

The first sightings

The story opens with the thing the realm does not believe in. Rangers of the Night’s Watch, north of the Wall in what should be the tail of a very long summer, find bodies arranged in a pattern that no wildling made, and meet what killed them; one man runs, is taken for a deserter, and is beheaded by Eddard Stark for telling the truth. Within months the dead are walking inside Castle Black itself, and a Lord Commander is nearly killed in his own chambers by a corpse he had laid out for burial.

The Watch’s response is the great ranging beyond the Wall, and it goes very badly — the expedition is broken at the Fist of the First Men, the Lord Commander is murdered by his own men at a mutiny in a wildling’s hall, and what limps back south is a fraction of what marched. Both tellings carry all of this, with the show compressing the ranging and cutting most of the Watch’s internal politics.

Nobody came

The letters went south. The Watch begged the Iron Throne, the Wardens and every great house for men, and got nothing worth having: a handful of prisoners, some polite regrets, and eventually the attention of a king who came north for his own reasons. Meanwhile the realm fought the war for the throne, the Riverlands were stripped bare, and armies that could have manned every castle on the Wall marched up and down the kingsroad destroying each other’s harvests.

The one southern power that took the north seriously was Stannis Baratheon, who marched his army to the Wall and broke a wildling host that no one else would have bothered to fight. He did it partly because a red priestess told him the true war was there, which is one of the very few occasions in the entire story where prophecy produced a correct decision in time to matter.

The Wall and the dead

On screen the story is finished and the shape of it is grim. The wildlings were let through the Wall by a Lord Commander who was murdered by his own officers for it. A dragon was killed beyond the Wall and raised by the enemy, and the Wall itself — thousands of years of ice and whatever was worked into it — came down at Eastwatch in a night. The dead marched south. What followed at Winterfell was the Long Night in miniature: one battle, in one night, that very nearly ended the human population of the north, and that ended when Arya Stark reached the Night King with a dagger.

It should be said that the show’s version has no counterpart in the books. There is no Night King on the page; the Others are barely described, have never been given a leader, and have not come south in force. The Wall stands. Whether the novels intend a single climactic night or a long grinding war nobody knows, and the television resolution — one battle, one enemy commander, one blow — is not evidence about what the novels will do.

Where the page stands

At the end of the published books the Long Night has not returned. Winter has been formally declared by the Citadel, which is the closest thing this world has to an official announcement of catastrophe. The Wall is held by a Watch in mutiny against itself. The wildlings are partly through, partly dead and partly still out there. Something is moving beyond the Wall in numbers that nobody has counted, and the men in the best position to count them are busy stabbing their Lord Commander in a courtyard.

The oldest warning in Westeros has therefore been half-vindicated on the page and fully vindicated on the screen, and in both cases the vindication arrived after the point at which anything could be done cheaply. The realm was told for eight thousand years. It heard it as a saying.
