House Targaryen

The dragonlords of old Valyria, silver-haired and violet-eyed, who alone survived the Doom and conquered the Seven Kingdoms astride dragons. A three-headed red dragon on black, they ruled from the Iron Throne for near three hundred years before the rebellion cast the last of them into exile across the narrow sea.

The fuller tale

Every other great house in Westeros is native to it; the Targaryens are not, and the whole of their history is the story of that difference never quite resolving. Dragonlords of Valyria, they moved their seat to lonely Dragonstone a century before the Doom swallowed the rest of their homeland whole — a stroke of caution or prophecy, depending which Targaryen you ask, that left them the last dragonriders alive when everything else Valyrian became ash and legend. Aegon the Conqueror crossed the narrow sea with two sisters, three dragons, and an army too small to matter against six kingdoms that dwarfed it — and won anyway, because none of those kingdoms had ever had to fight something that could burn an army from the sky. Fire and Blood is not phrased as a boast so much as a description of the terms.

What three centuries of Targaryen rule actually produced was less a golden age than an unbroken coin flip between brilliance and ruin, landing differently every generation. Aegon the Conqueror unified a continent by the sword and then, notably, stopped conquering when Dorne's deserts and spears made the cost too high — restraint being the one Targaryen habit that never seemed to run in the blood as strongly as its opposite. Jaehaerys the Old ruled fifty-five years of the closest thing Westeros ever had to lasting peace; his descendants set the bar for its opposite, burning rivals alive for imagined crimes, hearing prophecy in a screaming wind, and mistaking their own certainty for their ancestors' fire. The valyrian steel and stone eggs the family kept as heirlooms were not decoration. They were a reminder, generation after generation, of a power the family had once earned and only sometimes still deserved.

The dragons died out first, starved of purpose and inbreeding both, and the dynasty followed less than two centuries later. Robert Baratheon's rebellion did not need to defeat an empire so much as topple what the empire had already hollowed itself into — a mad king muttering about wildfire, a golden-haired heir the realm no longer trusted, and a war that ended at the Trident with a war hammer and a chest full of red rubies. What was left of House Targaryen afterward fit inside a single hunted family: a boy who called himself a beggar king, a girl not yet born, and three fossilized dragon eggs that every sane person in Westeros assumed were merely rocks.

That the tale's two central figures both turn out to be Targaryens — one raised as the last exile, one raised believing himself no Targaryen at all — is the family's oldest joke landing one final time. Both are shaped by exile and both are shaped by fire, and where the coin lands for each of them says less about their blood than about what they choose to do with the same inheritance. The three-headed dragon on black was always a family crest built for plurality — three heads, one banner — and by the story's end it has been carried, worn, hatched from and finally set down by more claimants than any single Targaryen king ever ruled alone.

The page and the screen

The show compresses three centuries of Targaryen history into implication and dialogue — Robert's Rebellion, the Mad King's burnings, even Aegon's conquest are told rather than shown, background the audience is trusted to infer. Fire & Blood, Martin's standalone Targaryen history, gives the dynasty's earlier centuries — the Dance of the Dragons, the Doom's long shadow, the slow extinction of the dragons themselves — a depth and specificity the main saga has not yet caught up to on the page, effectively meaning the books now contain more documented Targaryen history than either telling has dramatized in full. The house's living story, meanwhile, remains genuinely unfinished in print: Daenerys's arc in the published novels has not yet reached Westeros at all, let alone King's Landing, leaving the show's ending — however final it looked on screen — unconfirmed by the only author with the authority to write it.
