House Stark

Kings of Winter turned Wardens of the North, the Starks have ruled from Winterfell for eight thousand years ÔÇö since the Age of Heroes and Bran the Builder. Grey direwolves on white ice, they keep the old gods and the First Men's ways, ruling the largest and coldest of the Seven Kingdoms with a hard, plain northern honor.

The fuller tale

The Starks trace themselves back past written record to Bran the Builder in the Age of Heroes, and past him again into legend the Starks themselves no longer bother to defend or deny. What is certain is older than the Andals, older than the Seven: eight thousand winters of Kings of Winter ruling from Winterfell, a line that fought giants and the children of the forest to a standstill and then, uniquely among the First Men, made peace instead of war. That peace is why the Starks alone kept the old gods when the Andals came south with their new one, and why a heart tree still weeps in Winterfell's godswood while every other great house prays to statues.

Winterfell itself explains the family better than any genealogy. Built over hot springs that run warm water through its walls, it lets a Stark host a hall in furs while snow buries the yard outside ÔÇö a castle that turned the North's cruelty into comfort rather than pretending to escape it. The crypts beneath hold every King of Winter in stone, swords laid over their laps to keep old vengeance sleeping, and it is down among those tombs, unremarked for a generation, that the story keeps its best-hidden secret. From Winterfell the Starks hold the largest of the Seven Kingdoms ÔÇö bigger than the other six combined, mostly empty, entirely theirs ÔÇö through bannermen bound by a harder currency than affection: the Umbers, Karstarks, Mormonts and Boltons obey because the alternative is worse than obedience.

What holds across every generation is a specific, undecorated code. A Stark passes sentence himself and swings the sword himself, because a lord who cannot look his victim in the eye has no business owning the axe. Guests are fed and safe under a Stark roof regardless of the grudge outside it. None of this is boasted about ÔÇö the southern houses keep lion and stag and rose for their sigils and roar accordingly, while the Starks fly a direwolf on plain ice-white, an animal that hunts in silence and dies for its pack without asking permission first. Words of House Stark are not a promise of glory. Winter Is Coming is a weather report that happens to double as a warning: prepare now, because charm will not save you later.

Robert's Rebellion is fought in the Starks' name before it is fought for anyone's throne ÔÇö Rickard and Brandon Stark burned and strangled in King's Landing for a son's supposed insult, and Eddard Stark rides south a boy and comes home a lord with a war behind him and a secret he will keep for the rest of his life. That secret, and the honor that makes him keep it, cost him everything a generation later: the sept steps, the false confession, the axe he refused to swing on his own daughters' behalf. What follows is close to extermination ÔÇö Robb and Catelyn butchered at a wedding meant to buy peace, Winterfell burned twice over by men wearing borrowed names, the pack scattered to the corners of the map as prisoners, wards, fugitives and outlaws with nothing left of home but a memory.

The rebuilding is the part the family's whole history had been rehearsing for. Every child who survives comes back changed and useful in a way the North needs precisely because they left ÔÇö a queen's confidante, an assassin, a seer who remembers everything, a commander who has fought both living armies and dead ones. Winterfell is retaken at a bastard's cost and a sister's arithmetic, held through the Long Night in the same godswood where its secrets were buried, and by the story's end the North's independence is bought back by the very family whose extinction the war had seemed determined to finish. Eight thousand years is not a boast the Starks make. It is the answer to the question of why they were still there to win.

The page and the screen

The tellings part ways hardest at the finish. The show closes the Stark account neatly and fast ÔÇö Sansa crowned an independent Queen in the North within a single season of the war's end, Bran installed on the Iron Throne itself, Jon exiled north to the Watch and beyond it to the free folk, every thread visibly tied. The books, still marching through the War of the Five Kings' long aftermath as of the last published volume, have not caught up to any of it: the Others remain largely unengaged, Jon's fate hangs on an unresolved cliffhanger of his own, Arya's road home is unwritten, and an entire subplot the show skipped ÔÇö a captive steward's daughter passed off as Arya Stark to the Boltons, and Catelyn's grim second life as the vengeful, wordless Lady Stoneheart ÔÇö still waits in the wings of a story that has not decided how, or whether, to resolve them.
