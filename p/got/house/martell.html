House Martell

Princes of Dorne, the last kingdom to bow to the dragons — and only by marriage, never by conquest. A red sun pierced by a golden spear, the Martells rule the hot southern deserts from Sunspear by Rhoynish law and custom, patient, proud, and long of memory.

The fuller tale

Dorne is the one kingdom Aegon's dragons never conquered, and House Martell has built its entire identity around that fact ever since. When Nymeria, a Rhoynish warrior-queen fleeing Valyria's wars in the east, burned her ten thousand ships rather than let her people be used as a weapon again, she led them to the deserts of Dorne and married Lord Mors Martell to seal the union — bringing Rhoynish law with her into a kingdom the rest of Westeros had assumed would simply adopt Andal custom like everyone else. It didn't. Partible inheritance regardless of a child's sex, open acknowledgment of paramours and baseborn children, women who could rule, fight, and inherit on the same terms as men — Dorne kept its Rhoynish law under a Martell sun, and stayed visibly, deliberately different from the six kingdoms around it.

That difference is also why Dorne survived what no other kingdom did. Aegon's own sister Rhaenys flew Meraxes into the Dornish deserts expecting the same swift capitulation the rest of Westeros gave the Targaryens, and Dorne answered not with pitched battle but with heat, sand, and guerrilla patience — hiding, striking, vanishing, until dragons found nothing to burn and armies found nothing to conquer. Dorne did not bend the knee to House Targaryen for generations, and when it finally joined the realm it did so by marriage rather than surrender, under King Daeron the Second — the only great addition to the Seven Kingdoms that dragonfire never actually purchased. Unbowed, Unbent, Unbroken is not an idle motto in Dorne. It is a description of the only successful resistance in the Targaryen conquest's whole history.

The Red Viper carries that inheritance personally into the war generations later. Oberyn Martell, Prince Doran's fiery younger brother, arrives in King's Landing for Tyrion's trial with one purpose clear to everyone who has ever heard his family history: vengeance for his sister Elia, raped and murdered along with her children during the Sack of King's Landing, a crime the rest of the realm treated as regrettable collateral and Dorne never once forgot. His trial by combat against the man who killed her — fought with poison, pride, and a speech that names her murderer to his face before the killing blow lands — ends in the single most devastating reversal of fortune the tale ever stages, and Dorne's grief curdles, in its aftermath, from patience into open rage.

What follows in Dorne is a house divided against its own instincts. Prince Doran, who has spent decades playing a long, quiet game the rest of the realm mistook for weakness, finds his throne and his family's patience overtaken by relatives who want blood paid for blood immediately rather than on his careful schedule — his own niece and his dead brother's paramour among them. The kingdom that never bent to a dragon nearly breaks itself instead, from within, over exactly how much of its old grief it is willing to spend on new vengeance.

The page and the screen

Dorne is by consensus the most heavily altered storyline in the entire adaptation. The show compresses Dorne's slow-burning political tension into a single truncated arc — Ellaria Sand and the Sand Snakes poisoning Myrcella and seizing power in a palace coup, Doran and Trystane Martell killed off within an episode — that was widely regarded even at the time as the series' weakest stretch. The books have taken a far slower, more layered route that the show never adapted at all: Arianne Martell's secret plot to crown Myrcella queen of Dorne against her father's wishes, Doran's long-planned alliance with a mercenary company and a claimant to the Iron Throne raised in Essos, and a Dornish endgame that, as of the last published volume, has barely begun to reveal its actual shape.
