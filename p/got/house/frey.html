House Frey

Upjumped and enormously numerous, the Freys have held the only bridge across the Green Fork for six hundred years, growing rich on the tolls of the Twins. Two blue towers joined by a bridge on grey, they are bannermen to the Tullys — resented for their new blood and their habit of arriving late to every battle.

The fuller tale

Six hundred years of controlling the only bridge across the Green Fork of the Trident made House Frey rich in the one way that mattered most in the Riverlands: everyone who wanted to cross the river without swimming it owed the Freys a toll, a favor, or both. It also made them permanently unwelcome at the tables of older blood. The Twins is a castle built explicitly to be a chokepoint rather than a home, twin towers joined by a bridge, and the family that holds it has spent centuries being useful to its neighbors and resented by them in roughly equal measure — new money in a country that never forgets who used to be poor.

Walder Frey ran that inheritance the only way he knew how: by outliving nearly everyone and marrying eight times, accumulating a brood so enormous that no other house in Westeros could match it for sheer numbers, and holding every marriage as a debt the other party would eventually be made to pay. His bannermen obligation to House Tully brought his sons to Robb Stark's war reluctantly and late — late enough that Late Walder Frey became less an insult than an accepted fact about the man — and when Robb Stark broke a marriage pact made in Walder's hall to marry for love instead of alliance, Walder did not simply hold a grudge. He waited for the precise moment repayment would hurt the most.

The Red Wedding is the single worst violation of guest right the story ever stages, and it is entirely the Freys' architecture: a wedding feast, sacred hospitality invoked and then weaponized, music cutting off as a signal, a king and his mother and half his army slaughtered under a roof that was supposed to make them safe. It wins the war for the Lannisters and Boltons and it costs the Freys something no gold ever buys back — every house in the Seven Kingdoms now understands exactly what Frey hospitality is worth, and treats the name accordingly for the rest of the tale.

What survives the betrayal does not survive its consequences for long. The family that grew powerful by being everywhere, marrying everyone, and mattering to every alliance in the Riverlands discovers, once its name is poison, that numbers are no protection against an enemy patient enough to come for them one place setting at a time. House Frey's story ends the way it began — around a dinner table — except this time the Freys are the ones who trusted the wrong host.

The page and the screen

The show resolves the Freys' reckoning fast and total: Arya Stark, having taken a Frey woman's face, serves Walder Frey his own sons baked into a pie, kills him, and later poisons the assembled remainder of the family at a single feast, ending House Frey in the span of two seasons. The published books have not reached anything like that ending — Walder Frey is still alive as of the last volume, the family is fracturing from within over who inherits after his eventual death, and Riverrun's second siege leaves several Frey commanders and a captive Edmure Tully still very much in play. Where the show gave the Freys a clean, singular vengeance, the books are still tracking a slower, messier unraveling with no confirmed ending in sight.
