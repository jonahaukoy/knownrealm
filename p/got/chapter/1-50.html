The chapter retold

Robert Baratheon dies of his hunting wound, and in the throne room that follows, Ned makes his last honest play in a game he has never understood how to win dishonestly: he produces Robert's amended will naming him Protector of the Realm and declares, in front of the assembled court, what he has learned about Joffrey's true parentage, expecting the truth itself to be enough. Cersei tears the will in front of him without a flicker of hesitation, denying every word, and it is only then that Ned discovers how completely he has been outmaneuvered ÔÇö the gold cloaks he thought would back the Hand's authority answer to Littlefinger, and Littlefinger, who spent the whole book performing loyalty, sets a dagger to Ned's throat instead.

The betrayal is staged with a cruelty that lands harder for how quietly it's delivered: no grand speech, just Littlefinger's own words thrown back at Ned ÔÇö I did warn you not to trust me ÔÇö a warning Ned heard, filed away as charming cynicism, and never once believed applied to him personally. Ned's entire strategy in this chapter rests on the assumption that truth, properly stated in the proper venue, compels belief and obedience; Cersei and Littlefinger both understand, and Ned does not, that in King's Landing the truth only matters as much as the swords standing behind whoever's telling it.

This is the chapter where the book's title stops being a metaphor and starts being a body count. Robert's death ends the fragile peace his marriage to Cersei had papered over for seventeen years, and Ned's arrest ÔÇö engineered by the one man at court he never suspected, using the one instrument of force he thought was his ÔÇö removes the single character most committed to doing things the honest way, at the exact moment honesty stops being enough to survive on. Every war that follows in this book and the next traces back to this room, this torn will, and this dagger.
