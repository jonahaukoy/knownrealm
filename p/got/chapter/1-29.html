At the crossroads inn Tyrion Lannister walks in on Catelyn Stark; she calls on her father's bannermen and takes him ÔÇö but not toward Winterfell.

The chapter retold

Catelyn Stark, traveling in secret and meaning to slip quietly past the crossroads on her way home, instead finds herself face to face with Tyrion Lannister in the common room of a wayside inn ÔÇö the very man Littlefinger named as owner of the dagger sent to kill her son. Rather than let the moment pass, she stands on a bench and calls on the assembled lords and knights, bannermen and strangers to her father's House Tully, to help her take him prisoner in the king's name. Enough of them answer the call that Tyrion, badly outnumbered and with no way to fight free, is taken ÔÇö though not toward King's Landing, and not toward Winterfell either, but up into the Mountains of the Moon, to her sister Lysa's Eyrie.

It's an extraordinary piece of improvised political theater: Catelyn has no proof beyond Littlefinger's word, no warrant, no legal standing to arrest a Lannister on the open road, and she manufactures the authority to do it anyway out of pure nerve and the accumulated weight of her family's name. The men who answer her call do so because refusing a Tully-born lady's public appeal, in front of witnesses, would cost them more than the risk of crossing House Lannister ÔÇö a bet on honor culture that Catelyn wins in the moment and will spend the rest of the book paying for.

The consequences ripple outward from this single decision faster than almost anything else in the novel: Tywin Lannister will not sit still for his son's imprisonment, war edges measurably closer the instant word reaches Casterly Rock, and Catelyn's choice to divert to the Eyrie rather than face King's Landing directly buries Tyrion in her sister's court, where madness rather than law will decide his fate. Whatever the truth about the dagger turns out to be, Catelyn has just converted a private family tragedy into a matter of state.
