A Dance with Dragons — Cersei II

The walk: shorn and stripped, Cersei Lannister crosses King's Landing through filth and screaming — shame, shame, shame — to where a silent giant in white armor waits.

The chapter retold

Cersei’s confession comes due: shorn bald, stripped naked, she is marched the length of King’s Landing from the Great Sept back to the Red Keep while septas ring bells and the crowd she once ruled screams shame at her with an appetite that makes clear how much resentment had been banked against her all along. A broken man called the Blue Bard, tortured by the Faith until he’d say anything to make it stop, provided testimony enough to doom her — and finds, once the songs he once made his living from have stopped mattering to anyone, that his usefulness ends where hers begins.

The Walk is the book’s most sustained and pitiless physical ordeal, narrated with unflinching attention to every step, every jeer, every small cruelty a mob can inflict on a body it’s been given permission to hate — and it works precisely because the text refuses to let the reader enjoy Cersei’s downfall uncomplicatedly, forcing sympathy for a woman whose cruelties earned her enemies even as her punishment vastly outstrips any specific crime being punished. At the walk’s end a silent giant in white armor is waiting for her, and the chapter closes on the queen’s collapse into a mercy she has done nothing in this book to deserve and receives anyway.
