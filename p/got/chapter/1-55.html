The chapter retold

Drogo, honoring his fury over the wineseller's poison and the promise it provoked, turns his khalasar against the Lhazareen ÔÇö the peaceful sheep-herding people of the grass sea ÔÇö raiding their villages for the slaves he needs to trade for the ships that will carry his army across the narrow sea. Daenerys, horrified by the scale of what her husband's wrath has unleashed on people who did nothing to earn it, claims the surviving women for herself rather than let them be parceled out as spoils, using her authority as khaleesi in a way that startles Drogo's own bloodriders. In the chaos of the raid, a wound opens across Drogo's chest ÔÇö small, seemingly minor, the kind of scratch that wouldn't trouble most men.

Daenerys's intervention on behalf of the Lhazareen women is the clearest statement yet of the moral compass that will define her entire later reign ÔÇö an instinct to protect the powerless even when it costs her standing with the powerful, exercised here for the first time on a scale large enough to matter. The wound in Drogo's chest, mentioned almost in passing amid the larger drama of the raid, is the kind of detail this book plants without emphasis and lets the reader worry over on their own ÔÇö small enough to ignore, in exactly the way that gets ignored until it's too late.
