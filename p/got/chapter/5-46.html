A Dance with Dragons — Arya I

A blind girl begs on Braavos's canals and learns to see with skin and ears; each night the kindly man asks who she is, and each night the lie tastes safer: no one.

The chapter retold

In Braavos, a blind beggar girl works the canal-side crowds for coins and scraps, and readers who remember Arya Stark’s sharper self have to relearn her patience through months of sensory deprivation — the House of Black and White has taken her sight as tuition, and she is being taught, slowly and without mercy, to read a city through smell, sound, and touch instead. Each night the kindly man who oversees her training asks the same question — who are you? — and each night the answer she gives gets a little easier to say and a little less true.

The chapter is the clearest statement yet of what the Faceless Men actually want from Arya: not a killer, which she already half is, but an erasure, a girl willing to trade her name for power over other people’s names. Her blindness, cruel as it is, doubles as the book’s most literal metaphor for the whole training regimen — strip away the self, and see what’s left worth keeping. What’s left, stubbornly, keeps being Arya Stark, whether the House approves or not.
