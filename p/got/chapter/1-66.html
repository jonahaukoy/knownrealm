The chapter retold

On the steps of the Great Sept of Baelor, in front of the assembled city, Ned Stark confesses to treason he never committed ÔÇö the price Cersei and Varys extracted for Sansa's safety and his own passage to the Wall, a lie sworn publicly by the most honest man in the story to save his daughters from the consequences of his own downfall. Arya is in the crowd below, having slipped out of the Red Keep in Yoren's care, and she watches her father kneel and speak the words that are supposed to end this peacefully. Instead, Joffrey, delighting in the crowd's bloodlust and utterly indifferent to whatever bargain the adults around him struck, orders the headsman anyway. Ned Stark is beheaded with his own greatsword, Ice, in front of both his daughters and the whole city, and Yoren's hand clamps over Arya's mouth and drags her from the crowd before her scream can give her away ÔÇö 'boy,' he hisses, shoving her into the recruit column he's leading north, the disguise that will carry her out of King's Landing alive.

This is the single most consequential execution in the entire book, and Martin stages it not from Sansa's vantage, who is meant to watch, nor from Ned's own perspective, but from Arya's ÔÇö a child in the crowd, powerless, unable to do anything but watch and be dragged away before she can even finish the scream. The choice of viewpoint refuses the reader any of the comfort a more heroic framing might offer: there is no last-second rescue, no clever escape, just a promise broken by a boy king who was never bound by adult bargains in the first place, and a girl who will carry the sight of it for the rest of the book and the books after it.

Every plotline the novel has been quietly assembling detonates from this one moment. Robb's war, already underway, loses any chance of a negotiated peace the instant his father's head leaves his shoulders; Sansa is now entirely a hostage with no bargaining power left to protect; and Arya, renamed 'Arry' and marched north in Yoren's column, begins the journey that will strip away everything about her old identity except the sword her half-brother gave her as a joke. Ned spent the whole book insisting that honor and honesty were worth any price ÔÇö the book answers him, here, by making him pay the highest price it has, and showing that the world he was trying to protect his children in doesn't honor bargains made in good faith at all.
