A Feast for Crows — Jaime VI

The riverlands fold back into the king's peace keep by keep; his aunt Genna tells Jaime a hard truth — Tyrion was Tywin's son, and Jaime never was.

The chapter retold

Jaime's slow circuit of the riverlands continues, castle after castle folding back into the king's peace with more ink spent than iron — but the chapter's real weight lands in a private conversation with his aunt Genna Lannister, Tywin's sister, newly widowed by the war's arithmetic. Genna does not flatter him. She tells Jaime, gently and without cruelty, that of Tywin's three children it was Tyrion — mocked, disowned, blamed for Joanna's death every day of his life — who was truly their father's son: ruthless, brilliant, unforgiving of weakness, capable of building or burning a house down to its foundations. Jaime, the golden knight who spent a lifetime performing the role of heir, never actually wanted Casterly Rock, and Genna says so plainly, watching to see whether it lands.

It lands. The chapter recontextualizes the entire Lannister family dynamic mere chapters after the reader has watched Tyrion put a crossbow bolt through Tywin's chest — Genna's verdict suggests Tywin's fatal misjudgment of his own children was no incidental cruelty but a blind spot as deep as any in the book, mistaking the son who looked like him for the son who actually thought like him. For Jaime, already unmoored from the Kingslayer identity that used to explain him to himself, it is one more plank pulled out from under a life built on being Tywin's heir — a role, Genna implies, he was only ever cast in, and never suited for.

This movement: The Walk of the Lion
