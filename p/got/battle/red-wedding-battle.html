The Red Wedding

299 AC ÔÇö the War of the Five Kings

Not a battle. A massacre under guest right, which is worse.

Why it came to this

Robb Stark had won every battle he fought and was losing the war anyway. After the Blackwater the crown had the Reach behind it, Winterfell had been burned by an ally turned enemy, his brothers were believed dead, his sisters were in Lannister hands, and his own bannermen were beginning to count the cost. He needed to end the war with a stroke that restored the NorthÔÇÖs position, and the plan he settled on was to take Moat Cailin from the south and drive the ironborn out of his homeland. To do that he had to march an army back north. To do that he had to cross the Green Fork.

There is exactly one crossing of the Green Fork in that country, and House Frey owns it. The Freys had been bought once already, at the start of the war, when Catelyn Stark negotiated passage in exchange for a pledge that her son would marry one of Lord WalderÔÇÖs daughters. Robb then married a woman of his own choosing instead ÔÇö a Westerling girl of the westerlands in the novels, a Volantene healer in the show ÔÇö and in doing so handed the most reliably aggrieved man in Westeros a grievance he could dine out on for the rest of his life.

The Freys withdrew from the war. Without the crossing and without their strength RobbÔÇÖs campaign was finished, so an apology was arranged: RobbÔÇÖs uncle Edmure Tully would marry a Frey daughter in his place, the wedding would be held at the Twins, and the northern army would be permitted to cross afterward. Walder Frey accepted with a show of grumbling reluctance that everybody read as characteristic and nobody read as tactical. What was not visible from the outside was that Roose Bolton ÔÇö RobbÔÇÖs most senior northern commander, who had spent the war spending other menÔÇÖs soldiers and preserving his own ÔÇö had already opened a correspondence with Tywin Lannister, and that the terms of the arrangement were the North itself.

The ground and the hosts

The Twins is not a castle so much as a fortified bridge: two towers on opposite banks joined by a span over the river, each with its own gate and its own hall. It is a structure built entirely around control of movement. Nothing crosses without permission, and anything inside it can be divided from anything else by shutting a door.

The northern host camped on the near bank in the fields around the castle, in the ordinary sprawl of an army at rest at a wedding ÔÇö pavilions, cookfires, wine issued by the host, weapons stacked, horses picketed, sentries thinned because there was no enemy within a hundred leagues. RobbÔÇÖs direwolf, which had a reliable habit of objecting to people who meant his master harm, was penned separately on the pretext that it made the household nervous.

The party that entered the castle for the feast was small: the king, his mother, his wife on the screen, his uncles, and the senior lords and captains of the northern and riverland host. That is the whole point of the design. Almost every man capable of holding the army together was placed inside one room, under a roof belonging to somebody else, in courtesy dress, with their swords at the door.

Against them Walder Frey had his own household in numbers, the retinues of a very large family, and crossbowmen quietly seated in the musiciansÔÇÖ gallery above the hall. Outside, BoltonÔÇÖs men were camped among the northmen as comrades of two yearsÔÇÖ campaigning, positioned by unit rather than by chance, and the Frey levies had been drawn up within reach of the camps. Roose Bolton wore mail beneath his wedding clothes; on the page Catelyn Stark notices it a few moments too late to matter.

And over all of it lay guest right, the oldest law the North keeps ÔÇö that a man who has eaten his hostÔÇÖs bread and salt beneath his hostÔÇÖs roof is safe there, and a host who breaks it is cursed by gods old and new. Bread and salt were served. This was not an oversight. It was the mechanism. Guest right was the only thing disarming that hall, and the Freys served it deliberately, knowing what it bought them.

How it was fought

The wedding itself was performed properly, and the feast that followed was long, loud and dull in the way of feasts, which is what allowed the arrangements to be completed while everyone watched. The bride was bedded with the usual noise, which removed a large part of the company from the hall and left the remainder scattered, drunk and pleased with themselves. Then the doors were closed. In a hall with two hundred guests and one exit, closing the doors is the entire tactical plan.

The signal was the music. The musicians in the gallery ÔÇö who had been indifferent all evening, and who were not musicians ÔÇö struck up a song about a great house that had annihilated a lesser one for presuming to rival it. It is a Lannister song, and Tywin Lannister was two hundred miles away, and the choice of it was a message from him: this is what happens, and this is who arranged it. Anyone in that hall who knew the tune knew what it meant, but the crossbows in the gallery were already levelled by the time the meaning arrived.

The killing inside the hall was over quickly. Crossbows from a gallery into a seated, unarmoured, half-drunk company at fifteen feet do not leave much for swords to finish. There was no line, no melee, no possibility of rally; the men who mattered were dead or dying within a minute or two of the first bolt. Restraint is the right register here, so let the record simply say that the Frey household did the work with knives afterward and that the leadership of the northern army ceased to exist between one course and the next.

The second killing was the larger one and is the part usually forgotten. Simultaneously, outside the walls, the northern camps were attacked. Frey levies came at them from the castle side while the Bolton contingents already inside the encampment turned on the men beside them. An army asleep in its tents, its lords absent at a feast, its arms stacked, attacked at close quarters by troops it had been marching with for two years, has no defence at all ÔÇö there is nobody to give an order and nobody to form on. Some northmen got to horses. Most did not. The Stark host was not defeated in those camps; it was dismantled.

The one thing the plan could not control was the river. The Twins straddles it, and men who ran the right way and could swim went into the water in the dark and out the other side. The escapes were few and mostly accidental ÔÇö a commander who had stepped outside, a party that had not yet come up, individuals who happened to be beyond the gate. Everything within the trap was lost.

By dawn the Freys held the crossing, the Boltons held what remained of the northern army as their own, and the war in the riverlands had been decided without a battle being fought. It cost Walder Frey a reputation he had never much used and Roose Bolton nothing whatsoever.

What it cost

The North lost the greater part of a generation of fighting men in one night, and the great houses of the North lost the sons and heirs they had sent south. The riverlands lost the leadership of their rebellion. Riverrun was besieged and forfeited, the Tullys were dispossessed, and the war that had begun with a lord going south to serve as Hand of the King ended with his family holding nothing.

The Freys were made materially rich and socially radioactive in the same evening. They were given lands, a castle and a lordship; they also acquired a name that no house in Westeros would sit at table with, which is a real cost in a society that runs on hospitality. Guest right is not sentiment. It is the load-bearing custom of a country where every journey depends on a strangerÔÇÖs roof, and it had been broken publicly and cynically for money.

Roose Bolton got the North. Tywin Lannister got the war ended for the price of a letter, and said so plainly afterward: a dozen men at dinner is cheaper than a battle, and he considered anyone who found that objectionable to be confusing sentiment with arithmetic.

What it changed

Strategically the Red Wedding ended the northern rebellion outright and left the crown effectively unopposed in the south. It also removed the only army in Westeros that had been winning, at a moment when the realm was about to need one.

Politically it did something worse and slower. It taught every lord in the Seven Kingdoms that the oldest protections were negotiable if the payment was large enough, and it left the North occupied by a house it despised, garrisoned by a family it would not forgive. Occupations founded on an atrocity are cheap to establish and very expensive to keep.

It is also the moment the story stops pretending. Up to that night the war has the shape of a contest between a just cause and an unjust one. Afterwards the reader and the viewer both understand that the story is not that kind of story, and that competence, honour and the winning of battles are not what decides who ends up holding the ground.

What became of it ÔÇö if an ending is written, it is here. Spoilers.

Robb Stark died in that hall, and the crossbow bolts that opened the killing were meant for him from the first. His mother Catelyn died moments after him, having taken a hostage and found that Walder Frey did not value his own wife enough for it to work. Roose Bolton delivered the final blow to the king himself.

On the screen RobbÔÇÖs wife Talisa was present and was among the first killed, along with the unborn child. The novels are different in a way that matters: Jeyne Westerling was not brought to the Twins at all and survived the night, which leaves a loose thread the books have not yet pulled.

Grey Wind was penned away from his master precisely because he could not be fooled, and was shot in the kennels. The mutilations the Freys performed on the bodies afterward were a deliberate piece of political humiliation, and Westeros duly noticed.

Edmure Tully, whose wedding it was, was taken alive and held for years as a lever against Riverrun. The Blackfish escaped by an accident of timing, being outside the hall when the doors shut. Arya Stark and Sandor Clegane arrived at the gate as the camps were being destroyed and were turned away from it, which is very probably what saved her.

Roose Bolton was named Warden of the North and Walder Frey was given Riverrun. Neither man enjoyed the tenure for long, and the reckoning when it came was carried out by people the Freys did not think of as dangerous.

In the novels one further thing came out of the Green Fork that the screen omits entirely: something was pulled from the river days later by the brotherhood without banners, and what walks the riverlands afterward under the name Lady Stoneheart hangs Freys as a matter of policy.
