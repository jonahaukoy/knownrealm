Rook's Rest

129 AC — the Dance

A modest Crownlands castle where both sides learned that dragons die.

Why it came to this

The Dance of the Dragons opened as a paperwork war. Viserys I had named his daughter Rhaenyra heir and made his lords swear to her; on his death his widow’s faction crowned his eldest son Aegon instead, and for the first several months the fighting consisted of letters, arrests and the quiet business of finding out which castles would answer which raven. Both sides had dragons and neither side wanted to be the first to use them properly, because everyone with any sense understood what a dragon war would mean and hoped the other side would collapse before it came to that.

The greens held King’s Landing but not the sea approaches to it. Corlys Velaryon had taken his fleet over to the blacks, and with it the Gullet, which meant the capital could be starved on the crown’s own doorstep. Meanwhile the Crownlands were a patchwork: castles a day’s ride from the city had declared for Rhaenyra and were sitting there unmolested, which is intolerable for a new regime that needs to look inevitable.

Ser Criston Cole, Hand and commander of the green field army, went out to fix that. He took the small castles east of the city in turn, and the campaign had a second purpose that he did not advertise. Every castle he reduced was a demand for the blacks to respond. Dragonstone could not watch its declared friends be picked off one at a time and keep the friends it had left. Criston was not really besieging castles. He was fishing.

The ground and the hosts

Rook’s Rest is a modest seat on the Crownlands coast, held by a lord who had come out for Rhaenyra early and loudly, and it is not the sort of place a war turns on. It has walls, a keep and a good enough position to be annoying, and nothing else. What made it the right bait was distance: near enough to Dragonstone that help could arrive within the day, far enough from King’s Landing that help would not expect what was waiting.

The important terrain was not the castle at all but the country around it — wooded high ground within easy reach of the walls, and the sky above it. Criston brought a competent field army, siege equipment and, critically, scorpions and heavy bows sited to shoot upward rather than at the walls. He also brought no dragon of his own where anyone could see one.

The blacks’ council on Dragonstone argued about the response and, according to every account, argued badly. What was sent was Rhaenys Targaryen — the Queen Who Never Was, sixty-odd years old, a rider of enormous experience on Meleys, a red she-dragon fast enough to be worth a great deal more than her age suggested. Whether she flew out as one part of a considered plan, or because she took the decision herself when the council would not take one, is a point on which the surviving histories disagree in a way that flatters whichever party each historian favoured.

Concealed nearby, out of sight of both the castle and the sky, waited the other two. Aemond Targaryen had Vhagar, the last dragon of the Conquest, old, enormous and by a wide margin the most dangerous animal alive. King Aegon II had Sunfyre, a young dragon of great beauty and no great size. That both of them were present, and whether Aegon was expected to be, is another disputed point. Some accounts have Criston summoning the king; others have him arriving unannounced and unwanted, having decided that his war needed him to be seen in it.

How it was fought

The opening was an ordinary assault. Criston’s men went at Rook’s Rest in earnest — properly, not as a feint, because a feint would have been read as one. The garrison held the walls and sent up smoke, and within a few hours the answer came out of the east.

Rhaenys came in low over the besiegers and did what a dragon does to a siege line: she broke it open along its length, scattered the assault, and set the siege works burning. For a short interval the battle was simply over. A single dragon committed against unprepared infantry in the open is not a contest, and the accounts of this phase read like the Field of Fire in miniature. Criston’s host took real losses here, and he let it happen, which tells you what the losses were for.

Then the trap closed. Vhagar and Sunfyre came up out of concealment and took Meleys from two sides at once. This is the moment the entire war pivots on, and it is worth being precise about why it worked. Dragons in the air are not soldiers in a line; a dragonrider’s advantage is height, speed and the ability to choose the pass. Rhaenys had spent hers coming down onto the siege lines. She was low, slow and committed, with an enemy above her and an enemy behind her, and there was no altitude left to trade for escape.

Whether she could have run is the great argument about Rook’s Rest. Meleys was the faster animal and open sky lay to the east. One tradition holds that she was pinned before she understood what was happening and had no choice at all. Another insists she saw both of them, understood the arithmetic exactly, and turned into them anyway — on the reasoning that if the greens were going to have their king and their best rider in one place, this was the only chance anyone would ever get to remove both. The two readings produce very different women and the sources do not settle it.

What is not disputed is that she fought. Meleys went at them rather than away, and the three animals came together in a tangle that dropped out of the sky as one mass. The fighting from that point was not aerial manoeuvre but something closer to a dogfight on the ground: claws, teeth and fire at contact range, with the riders along for it. Vhagar’s size was decisive. Weight tells in a grapple, and Vhagar was heavier than the other two together.

Sunfyre came out of it wrecked — a wing ruined, unable to fly, and the king with him. Aegon II fell a considerable distance in the collapse and was picked out of the wreckage burned across much of his body with the bones on one side broken. Whether Aemond struck at his own brother in the confusion, or whether Aegon simply flew into the path of a fight he had no business joining, is the single most contested question of the whole war. The chroniclers of the day mostly ascribed it to accident; one notorious and unreliable source insisted it was deliberate; on the screen it is staged as an unmistakable choice. The honest answer is that only one man knew and he never said.

With the sky cleared, the assault on the castle resumed and Rook’s Rest was carried by storm before the day was out. Criston had his castle. That was never the point of the day and everyone involved knew it.

What it cost

Meleys was destroyed — the first dragon in a hundred and fifty years to be killed by another dragon, and the moment the war stopped being a dispute and became something the whole realm should have been terrified of. The head was carried into King’s Landing on a cart and shown to the crowds, which was intended as reassurance and functioned, for anyone paying attention, as an announcement that dragons were mortal.

The green host lost heavily in the opening phase — Criston paid a real price in men for the bait, and it went unremarked because the bait worked. The garrison and household of Rook’s Rest were destroyed with the castle when it fell, and the castle itself was left a shell for years.

The king’s injuries were the most consequential casualty of the day. Aegon II was carried back to the capital insensible, and he remained incapable for most of the rest of the war. Sunfyre never properly flew again in the sense of being a weapon, though he crawls back into the story later in a way nobody expected.

The man made by Rook’s Rest was Aemond. With his brother out of action and the realm’s largest dragon under him, he became the effective power in King’s Landing and stayed there. The man made and then quietly ruined was Criston Cole, who had conceived and executed the most successful operation of the war and got, for it, a crippled king and a colleague who no longer needed him.

What it changed

Rook’s Rest is where the Dance turns from a succession dispute into a war of mutual destruction. Before it, both sides had dragons and both sides were, in effect, holding them back as deterrents. Afterwards the deterrent was gone, because it had been proved that a dragon could be trapped and killed by other dragons if you were willing to arrange it. Everything that follows — the hunts, the ambushes, the dragonseeds put into saddles by people who should have known better — proceeds from that demonstration.

It also changed the leadership of both sides at a stroke, one by injury and one by loss. The blacks lost their most experienced rider and, with her, a certain amount of restraint at their council table. The greens lost a king to his bed and replaced him in practice with a one-eyed nineteen-year-old whose approach to policy was to burn whatever had annoyed him most recently.

And it hardened a habit of mind that the war never recovered from: the belief on both sides that the answer to the other side’s dragon was your own dragon. That is the thinking that carries them all the way to the Gods Eye and the empty sky afterwards.

What became of it — if an ending is written, it is here. Spoilers.

Rhaenys Targaryen died on that field with Meleys. Whether she fell in the air or in the wreck on the ground is not agreed; the accounts that had access to the body are silent on the manner and enthusiastic about the fact. She had been passed over for the throne once by a Great Council and once by the terms of her cousin’s will, had watched her own children’s inheritance go elsewhere, and had gone on fighting for her house anyway. She was the most competent commander either side possessed and neither side had listened to her.

Aegon II survived his burns but never fully recovered from them; he spent months insensible, ruled in name only while his brother ruled in fact, and remained in pain for the rest of a reign that had barely started.

The dispute over whether Aemond fired on his brother deliberately was never settled and became, in the years after the war, the standard example maesters use when teaching that eyewitness testimony and political convenience are hard to tell apart.
