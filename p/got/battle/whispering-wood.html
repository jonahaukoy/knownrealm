The Whispering Wood

298 AC — the War of the Five Kings

The night a sixteen-year-old boy trapped the finest swordsman in the realm in a wood.

Why it came to this

Eddard Stark had gone south as Hand of the King and had been arrested for treason. In the north his eldest son called the banners, and what marched out of Winterfell was an army raised by a boy who had never commanded anything, on behalf of a father who was already beyond helping. Robb Stark’s stated war aim at this point was extremely limited: get his father and sisters back. Everything else that follows is improvisation.

The strategic position was bad. Two Lannister armies were in the field. Ser Jaime Lannister had come up from the west, smashed the Tully host in the open, and was besieging Riverrun with a large army split into three separate camps around the castle — Robb’s grandfather inside, his uncle a prisoner, the riverlands being stripped. Lord Tywin Lannister was further east with the larger and more dangerous host, positioned to catch anything coming down the kingsroad. Robb had one army and two enemies, and moving against either exposed him to the other.

He also had a river problem. To reach the riverlands at all he had to cross the Green Fork, and the only crossing was the Twins, held by Lord Walder Frey, an old man with a large family, a strong position and no intention of committing to anybody’s war for free. The price of the crossing was negotiated, and the terms included marriages — a promise made by a boy in a hurry, of a kind that seems cheap at the time.

Once across, Robb did the thing that made his reputation. Rather than choose between his two enemies, he decided to fight both at once with one army by dividing it and lying about which half mattered.

The ground and the hosts

The Whispering Wood is a wooded valley near Riverrun, close enough to the castle that a force operating from the siege lines could ride into it and back in a night. Its shape is the whole battle: a bowl with high wooded sides and one usable route through the bottom. Cavalry that enters it in column cannot deploy, cannot see and cannot easily turn round. It is, in short, the best ambush ground within reach of the siege, and Robb had local men who knew it and Jaime did not.

The Stark host was divided at the Twins. The larger part, mostly foot, went east up the kingsroad under Roose Bolton with orders that amounted to making a great deal of noise and not winning. Robb took the horse — a few thousand of them, the accounts vary — west and south, moving fast, hiding his numbers, and cutting off every scout and outrider the enemy sent. On the page the diversionary force is the greater part of the army and is meant to survive; on the screen Robb speaks of sending two thousand men to their deaths, which is more dramatic and rather less like the plan that was actually executed. Either way, its purpose was identical: to persuade Tywin Lannister that the northern army was in front of him.

It worked because of the outriders. The single most important tactical act of the campaign was unglamorous — the systematic killing or capture of every enemy scout in the region, so that the Lannister commanders were operating blind while Robb was not. The direwolf that ran with the Stark van has a good deal to do with why the enemy scouts kept not coming back.

Jaime Lannister was, at this point in his life, an outstanding fighter and an indifferent general. He was besieging Riverrun with his army in three camps, which is sound practice for investment and appalling practice if anybody attacks you, since no one camp can reinforce another quickly. When word reached him that a northern force was in the neighbourhood, he did not believe it could be the main host — that was supposed to be up the kingsroad in front of Lord Tywin — and he rode out with a body of horse to deal with what he assumed was a raiding party.

How it was fought

It was fought at night, in trees, in the dark, which is the reason no coherent narrative of it exists and why every retelling is a collection of fragments. Robb had his horse dismounted and waiting on both slopes of the valley, with archers above them, and had left the floor of the wood empty and inviting.

The Lannister column came in along the bottom in the dark, strung out, in no formation to speak of because there was no formation the ground would permit. The opening was a signal — horns from the high ground on both sides at once — and then arrows down into a column that could not see where they were coming from and had no shieldwall because it was riding, not fighting.

The first shock came from the slopes. Men and horse came down into the valley floor from two directions simultaneously, and hit a column that was already halted and already taking casualties. The specific tactical fact that decided it was the geometry: a mounted column in a defile has its strength in its length and its weakness everywhere else, and once the head and tail are both engaged the middle cannot get out. Jaime’s men were not beaten by better soldiers. They were beaten by being unable to become an army before the fight was over.

The moment it turned — insofar as a battle that was decided before it started can be said to turn — was Jaime’s own response, which was characteristic and very nearly effective. Rather than try to extricate the column, he identified the enemy commander’s position and drove straight at it with everything he could gather, on the reasoning that if he could reach a sixteen-year-old boy in the dark the whole thing would come apart. He got a long way. Men of Robb’s own guard went down stopping him, and the accounts agree that he was within a short distance of the boy when he was finally pulled off his horse and buried under numbers.

After that it collapsed quickly. A force that has lost its commander in a place it cannot retreat from does not conduct a fighting withdrawal; it dissolves into individual men trying to find a way out of a wood in the dark. The pursuit was mostly a matter of collecting them. A large number were taken alive, which mattered enormously — prisoners of rank are currency, and Robb had just acquired the most valuable single piece of currency in Westeros.

The wood was only half of it. At first light the Stark host came out of the trees and fell on the siege camps around Riverrun, which were now leaderless and had no idea what had happened in the night, while the garrison sortied out of the castle into their rear. The camps, unable to support one another by design, were destroyed one after another. The siege of Riverrun ended inside a day.

What it cost

The Stark losses in the wood were remarkably light for a battle of that consequence — the advantage of ambushing a column at night is that most of the killing is done by people who are not being shot at. They were not nothing, and the men who were lost were lost in the worst place: several of the young lords riding in Robb’s own guard went down in front of Jaime Lannister’s charge, and they were the heirs of houses that had followed the Starks south out of loyalty. Robb had to write those letters at sixteen.

The Lannister loss was catastrophic and mostly consisted of men taken rather than men killed. The besieging army was destroyed as a formation, a great many knights and lords of the west went into captivity, and the west’s campaign in the riverlands stopped dead.

The house made was Stark. Robb went into that wood as a boy whose banners had been called for him and came out of it as a commander that grown men would follow anywhere. Within days the riverlords in his camp had proclaimed him a king, which was not his idea and which he did not refuse.

The man ruined, temporarily, was Jaime Lannister — taken alive, held for a year and more, and reduced from the most feared sword in the realm to a bargaining counter in a tent. What that does to him is the most interesting thing that ever happens to the character, and it starts in this wood.

What it changed

It broke the Lannister strategy in the riverlands in one night. Tywin Lannister, who had spent the campaign manoeuvring against a diversion, found that the real northern army was behind him, his son’s army did not exist any more, and his line of communication to the west was compromised. He withdrew east toward Harrenhal, which is a considerable admission from a man who did not make them.

It also transformed the war politically. Up to this point Robb Stark had been a loyal lord’s son trying to free his father. After the Whispering Wood and the camps he was undefeated in the field, held the king’s uncle prisoner, and had the riverlords in his debt — and men in that position get offered crowns. The declaration of an independent north and riverlands is a direct consequence of a battle fought in the dark by a boy who had wanted a prisoner exchange.

And it established the pattern that the rest of Robb Stark’s war follows: he wins his battles and finds that winning them settles less each time, because battlefield success and political position are not the same currency. The Whispering Wood is the purest demonstration of what he could do with an army. It is also the campaign in which he mortgaged a promise to buy a bridge, and promises of that kind are held by people who keep accounts.

What became of it — if an ending is written, it is here. Spoilers.

The men of Robb’s guard cut down in front of Jaime Lannister’s charge included Daryn Hornwood and Torrhen and Eddard Karstark, sons and heirs of two of the great northern houses. Lord Rickard Karstark lost two sons in that wood in the space of a few minutes and never afterwards regarded the war, or Jaime Lannister, or in the end Robb Stark himself, as anything but a debt owed to him. That grievance festers for two more years and detonates in a way that costs Robb half his cavalry at the worst possible moment.

Jaime Lannister survived captivity, and the person who eventually came out of it was not the person who went in.

The bridge that made the whole campaign possible was bought with a marriage promise to House Frey. Robb Stark did not keep it. Lord Walder Frey, an old man with a long memory and no interest whatever in being laughed at, waited two years and settled the account at a wedding in his own hall, with the northern army inside his walls and bread and salt already served. Everything Robb won in the Whispering Wood was undone in a single evening by a debt he incurred on the way to it.
